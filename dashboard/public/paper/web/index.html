Summary

The Budget of 28 October 2026 adopted the government’s plan, announced on 29 September 2026, to adjust the State Pension triple lock: from April 2030 the State Pension rises “by the highest of 2.5%, inflation, or the amount required to maintain its value relative to earnings as reached in April 2029” (HM Treasury 2026). We compare this adjusted Triple Lock with the triple lock (current law) from 2027-28 to 2039-40, using full PolicyEngine UK runs of the whole survey population on the Budget’s inputs.

What it saves. The answer depends on the path of prices and earnings, so we report four scenarios and no expected value. On the OBR’s October 2026 central forecast the adjusted Triple Lock lowers State Pension spending by £0.5bn in 2039-40 (£0.3bn once income tax and benefits respond). If, instead, the triple lock outpaces earnings as the OBR’s long-term assumption has it, the saving is £10.9bn (£7.3bn net). Averaged over 160 simulated paths with earnings growth 0.5 points lower or higher from 2031, it is £11.4bn ± £0.5bn or £11.9bn ± £0.5bn (£7.8bn ± £0.3bn or £8.1bn ± £0.3bn net). These four figures bound a scenario envelope; they are not a probability interval. The government’s own figure, from DWP, is £15 billion a year for Great Britain by the late 2030s.

Why the path matters. The adjusted Triple Lock saves money only after years in which prices or the 2.5% floor beat earnings, as its level returns to its earnings path. On the OBR’s smooth central forecast earnings lead in almost every year, so the saving is small; on simulated paths prices and earnings swap the lead often, so they save far more.

Why no expected value. Our model of prices and earnings failed a test on past OBR forecasts that we registered before scoring it: it expected earnings growth below 2.5% in 44.6% of the years tested, against 17.9% in practice. Under the registered rule the paper therefore reports scenarios only.

Who is affected. About 30% of UK households, nearly all of them with a pensioner, have less income than under the triple lock in 2039-40 on every path: by £35 a year on average on the OBR central forecast and £801 on the OBR long-term path. On the OBR long-term path pensioner poverty after housing costs is up to 0.7 percentage points higher than under the triple lock.

1 Introduction

Since April 2011 the triple lock has raised the State Pension each April by the highest of prices, earnings growth and 2.5% (prices measured by RPI in its first year and by CPI since). Because it takes the highest of three figures every year, a year in which prices or the floor run ahead of earnings raises the pension for good, and later rises build on the higher level. The OBR’s long-term projections assume that the triple lock raises the pension 0.6 percentage points a year faster than earnings (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026c), and it estimates that by 2029-30 the triple lock will have added £15.5 billion a year to spending relative to earnings uprating since 2012 (Office for Budget Responsibility 2025).

The government announced on 29 September 2026 that it would keep the triple lock until 2030 and then adjust it, and DWP published an analysis of the change the same day (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b). The Budget of 28 October 2026 adopted it as the adjusted Triple Lock: from April 2030 the State Pension rises “by the highest of 2.5%, inflation, or the amount required to maintain its value relative to earnings as reached in April 2029” (HM Treasury 2026, para. 5.23 and Box 5.A). The Budget adds that “It removes the ‘ratchet’ effect, whereby the State Pension rises ever higher relative to earnings” (HM Treasury 2026, ch. 1), and puts the saving at: “Adjusting the Triple Lock is estimated to reduce State Pension spending by £15 billion a year by the late 2030s, and by £50 billion a year by the late 2040s” (HM Treasury 2026, para. 1.29).

This paper asks what the adjusted Triple Lock would save against the triple lock, and who would bear the difference. It makes three contributions. First, it shows that the saving depends on how prices and earnings move from year to year, not only on their averages: the rule saves money only after years in which prices or the floor beat earnings, so a costing on one smooth central path, which has almost no such years, finds almost no saving. Second, it costs the rule with full microsimulation runs of the whole survey population, so that every figure includes the response of income tax, Pension Credit and other benefits. Third, it tests its model of prices and earnings on past OBR forecasts under a rule set before scoring, reports that the model failed, and shows what that failure leaves: a scenario envelope rather than an expected value.

On the OBR’s central forecast the adjusted Triple Lock saves £0.5bn in State Pension spending in 2039-40. On the OBR’s long-term assumption about the triple lock it saves £10.9bn, and averaged over simulated paths with earnings growth moved 0.5 points either way it saves £11.4bn to £11.9bn. The cost to households falls almost entirely on pensioners, with Pension Credit and income tax making up part of it.

Section 2 sets out the two rules and where the saving comes from. Section 3 gives the four scenarios and other published costings. Section 4 follows both rules along the OBR’s forecast and simulated paths. Section 5 turns to households. Section 6 describes the method, the test on past forecasts and the rule for future releases, Section 7 the limitations, and Section 8 concludes.

2 The triple lock and the adjusted Triple Lock

2.1 The two rules

Under the triple lock the full basic and new State Pension rise each April by the highest of three figures: the CPI 12-month rate for the previous September (Office for National Statistics 2026c); growth in average weekly earnings, whole-economy total pay, in May to July of the previous year (Office for National Statistics 2026b); and 2.5%. ONS publishes both inputs to 0.1 point, and both rules here use them at that precision.

The adjusted Triple Lock follows the triple lock to April 2029. From April 2030 let LtL_t be the pension’s level and AtA_t an earnings path that starts at the 2029-30 level and grows with May–July earnings, At=At−1(1+et−1)A_t = A_{t-1}(1 + e_{t-1}). Then

Lt=max⁡(Lt−1(1+max⁡(ct−1,f)),At),L_t = \max\bigl(L_{t-1}\,(1 + \max(c_{t-1}, f)),\; A_t\bigr),

where cc is September CPI, ee is May–July earnings growth and ff is the 2.5% floor. The pension never rises by less than the higher of CPI and the floor, and never falls below its earnings path. In April 2030 this rise equals the triple lock’s, since the earnings path starts at the same level, so the two rules can first differ in April 2031 and 2030-31 saves nothing on any path. As a modelling assumption we round any top-up to the earnings path up to the next 0.1 point, so the pension never ends below that path; this favours the pensioner by at most 0.1 point a year.

The Budget’s wording fixes the anchor: the pension’s value relative to earnings “as reached in April 2029”. DWP’s description says the same in other words: the pension will “reach a record high relative to earnings over the coming three years”, and from April 2030 it keeps pensioners benefiting “via the State Pension retaining that record high value relative to earnings”: “In any given year it will go up by at least inflation or 2.5% – and anything more that is needed to retain that value”. It adds: “This means that over time the State Pension is set to rise in line with average earnings” (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b). Two choices remain ours. The rule uses the triple lock’s measures, September CPI and May–July earnings, and the top-up is rounded up, as above.

The law requires only that the basic and new State Pension rise at least in line with earnings (Social Security Administration Act 1992); the prices leg and the floor are policy on top. In a year when the adjusted Triple Lock rises by the higher of CPI and the floor while its level returns towards its earnings path, its rise is below earnings growth, which section 150A does not allow, so the rule would need primary legislation, as the suspension of the earnings leg for April 2022 did (Social Security (Up-rating of Benefits) Act 2021). On both OBR scenarios this happens once, in April 2032; on the two simulated paths shown in Section 4, in four and five of the Aprils from 2031 to 2039.

2.2 Where the saving comes from

When earnings lead, both rules give the same rise. The rules part only after a year in which the higher of CPI and 2.5% beats earnings. Both pay that higher figure, but it lifts the adjusted Triple Lock above its earnings path. When earnings lead again, the triple lock rises with earnings from the higher level, while the adjusted Triple Lock rises only by the higher of CPI and the floor until its earnings path catches up. Under the triple lock each such rise stays in the pension’s level; under the adjusted rule the level returns to its earnings path over the following years.

A two-year example shows the arithmetic. Suppose CPI is 6% and earnings growth 2% in the first year, then CPI 2% and earnings 4%. The triple lock rises by 6% and then 4%, 10.24% in all. The adjusted Triple Lock also rises by 6% in the first year, which leaves it above its earnings path, and then by the floor, 2.5%, 8.65% in all. It ends 1.4% below the triple lock (1.59 points less cumulative growth).

So the saving is zero on any path on which earnings lead in every year after the switch, and positive on a path on which the higher of CPI and the floor leads in some year and earnings lead again later. It depends on how often the lead passes between earnings and the higher of prices and the floor, not only on their average growth. The triple lock compounds each year’s highest input, so its level over uncertain inputs exceeds its level on the average inputs (Jensen’s inequality). The adjusted rule’s level is tied to an earnings path that does not compound those highs. A smooth central path has few changes of lead by construction; simulated paths, like past years, have many more (Section 6).

2.3 The triple lock since 2011

Figure 1 shows the rises actually paid in each April from 2011 to 2026. April 2011’s rise, 4.6%, was set by RPI under that year’s up-rating order (Social Security Benefits Up-rating Order 2011), while the replay of the CPI rule gives 3.1%. From 2012, CPI set the rise five times, earnings six times and the floor four times. In April 2022 the law suspended the earnings leg, whose input was 8.4% on today’s figures, so that year’s rise was CPI, 3.1% (Social Security (Up-rating of Benefits) Act 2021). Over the 16 rises the pension rose by 89.4% as paid.

The rest of this section replays the rule on today’s revised ONS figures, so that both rules see the same inputs. The replay differs from the rise paid in April 2016, 2019, 2020 and 2025, by 0.1 to 0.3 point, because earnings figures have since been revised, and in April 2011. On the replay the pension rose 87.4%, against 66.2% for earnings alone and 60.2% for prices alone, which leaves it 12.7% above where an earnings link alone would have taken it.

2.4 If the rule had started earlier

The same comparison can be run backwards on the replayed inputs. Had the adjusted Triple Lock started in April 2012, 2013, 2014 or 2015, its level in 2026-27 would be 6.2% below the triple lock’s on the same inputs (Figure 2). Applied to the full new State Pension actually paid, £12,548 a year, that would be £11,769. Most of the difference follows April 2023, when September CPI of 10.1% set a rise far above earnings growth of 5.5%. Started in April 2024, 2025 or 2026, the rule would have paid the same as the triple lock every year. In full model runs, State Pension spending in 2026-27 would have been £7.8bn lower, and the government £5.6bn better off once tax and benefits respond. Started in April 2017, a decade before the last rise, it would leave the pension 5.1% lower by 2026-27. The OBR replays the rule on outturn from 1990 to 2025: “Averaging over the period as a whole, state pension payments would have been on average 1.4 per cent lower each year than under the current triple lock and 4.4 per cent higher than under earnings uprating” (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a, para. 3.51).

3 What the adjusted Triple Lock would save

3.1 Four scenarios

Table 1 and Figure 3 give what the adjusted Triple Lock saves on four scenarios. Two are single full runs. One is the OBR central forecast. The other, the OBR long-term path, keeps the central forecast’s inputs but pays the triple lock at the OBR’s long-term triple-lock rate from April 2032, which phases in to earnings growth plus 0.6 points, and applies the adjusted rule to the same inputs. The other two take 160 simulated paths of prices and earnings, each a full run under both rules, and move calendar-year earnings growth 0.5 points a year lower or higher from 2031; each figure is the average over those paths, and its margin is the 95% Monte Carlo error from sampling them. We attach no probability to any of them, and the four do not form a probability interval: the registered rule withdrew the average over the unshifted paths (Section 6.4).

The OBR long-term path saves £10.9bn in 2039-40, £7.3bn once income tax and benefits respond: extra Pension Credit offsets £0.8bn and lower income tax £2.4bn. In this scenario earnings lead CPI and the floor in every April from 2032, so the adjusted Triple Lock returns to its earnings path and then follows it, and the whole of the OBR’s premium counts as saving. The premium is the OBR’s average allowance for years in which CPI or the floor beat earnings; no path of CPI and earnings pays it every year, so this run is a comparison with the OBR’s costing convention, not a forecast. The two earnings scenarios save £11.4bn and £11.9bn. Higher earnings growth raises the saving through the mid-2030s, £4.4bn against £3.1bn in 2034-35, and by 2039-40 the two are about £0.5bn apart.

3.2 Why the OBR’s central forecast saves little

The OBR’s central forecast is one smooth path. On it earnings beat CPI and the floor in almost every year after the switch, so the two rules give nearly the same rise: the two first differ in April 2032, and the adjusted Triple Lock saves £0.4bn in 2034-35 and £0.5bn in 2039-40 (£0.3bn net). The OBR finds the same for its forecast period: “in our current medium-term forecast an uprating of 2.5 per cent would apply under both the previous and adjusted version of the triple lock in both 2030-31 and 2031-32” (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a, para. 3.50). Real years are bumpier. Whenever inflation spikes or earnings growth dips below the floor, the triple lock keeps the higher rise for good while the adjusted rule’s level later returns to its earnings path, so the saving grows. The saving is never negative, and the central path has fewer such years than the simulated paths, so its saving is far below theirs.

3.3 Other costings

DWP costs the adjusted Triple Lock at £15bn in 2039-40 in cash terms (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b), and the Budget repeats that figure (Section 1). It is direct spending for Great Britain, rounded to the nearest billion, from an uprating path that DWP does not publish, projected with its dynamic microsimulation model of the population and individual pension outcomes; it leaves out tax and debt interest. Our nearest figures are the gross savings in Great Britain: £0.5bn on the OBR central forecast, £10.6bn on the OBR long-term path, and £11.1bn and £11.6bn on the two earnings scenarios. The comparison is not like for like. In answer to a parliamentary question, the government said that DWP’s analysis did not use single-year earnings or prices growth assumptions (UK Parliament 2026), so it does not correspond to any one of our paths. The population also differs: DWP projects deaths, migration and State Pension entitlements, while we reweight today’s survey (Section 6.3), whose State Pension spending in Great Britain is 98% of DWP’s forecast in 2026-27 (Table 8). DWP itself cautions against reading single-year point estimates from its figures, and its saving keeps growing, to £50bn in 2049-50; our horizon ends in 2039-40. DWP also gives £11bn in 2025-26 prices; our figures are in cash terms.

DWP also projects pensioner relative poverty after housing costs under the adjusted Triple Lock falling from around 14% to around 8% by 2049-50, against a poverty line projected forward with earnings. Our poverty rates (Section 5) are for 2039-40, use a line set each year from the simulated incomes, and compare the adjusted Triple Lock with the triple lock rather than with today, so they answer a different question.

Other published figures compare the triple lock with earnings uprating rather than with this rule, so they are context, not checks (Table 2). Our backward run, the adjusted Triple Lock from April 2012, is the nearest we have to those counterfactuals; it is a different rule from earnings uprating, since it keeps the price and floor protection.

4 One path in detail

4.1 The OBR central forecast

The central path uses the OBR’s October 2026 Economic and fiscal outlook, published with the Budget, for calendar years to 2030 (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a), and the OBR’s March 2026 long-term economic determinants after that (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026c), which the OBR describes as a projection, not a forecast; the OBR published no long-term determinants with its October forecast. April 2027’s State Pension rise uses the published May–July 2026 earnings growth, 3.9%, and September 2026 CPI, 3.5% (Office for National Statistics 2026b, 2026c). For the Aprils 2028 to 2031 the inputs are the OBR’s September-quarter CPI and April–June earnings growth; later Aprils use its calendar-year figures. The OBR forecasts average earnings on a national-accounts measure, which stands in here for May–July average weekly earnings. Both rules share these inputs.

The floor sets the triple lock in April 2029, 2030 and 2031; from April 2032 earnings lead every year (Figure 4). The two rules differ in one year, April 2032: after the floor years the adjusted Triple Lock rises only by the floor, or by what brings it back to its earnings path, while the triple lock rises with earnings from the higher level. By 2039-40 the full new State Pension is £18,876 a year under the triple lock and £18,840 under the adjusted rule, and the adjusted rule saves £0.5bn (£0.3bn net).

4.2 Simulated paths

The simulated paths come from a monthly model of prices and earnings, shifted so that their calendar-year averages match the OBR’s forecast (Section 6). The results file runs three of them in full: one drawn at random, and two picked for the size of the gap between the rules they open by 2039-40, one middle-sized and one large. Each is one path the model makes, not a forecast, and carries no probability weight. Figure 5 shows how far apart the two rules end on each, beside the two OBR scenarios, and Table 3 what each saves. The path with a middle-sized gap saves £11.3bn in 2039-40 (£7.7bn net), close to the OBR long-term path; the path with a large gap saves £19.8bn. Most of a path’s saving comes from a few years of high inflation or weak earnings, after which the gap stays.

5 Households

The household results run each path through PolicyEngine UK for the whole survey population, once under each rule. Every pensioner’s State Pension is recalculated, and with it their income tax, Pension Credit, Housing Benefit, council tax reduction and Winter Fuel Payment. We report them on the OBR long-term path, the largest of the single runs, and give the poverty changes on the OBR central forecast and the two simulated paths beside it. Cash and percentage changes in income scale roughly with the gap in the pension; poverty rates do not.

5.1 From gross to net

Table 4 follows the gross saving on the OBR long-term path to the net saving. In 2039-40 extra Pension Credit offsets £0.81bn of the £10.87bn gross saving and lower income tax £2.39bn, leaving £7.34bn net, 68% of the gross. The net figure counts direct taxes and benefits only, with no change in behaviour. It leaves out VAT and other indirect taxes on pensioners’ lower spending, which would make it smaller. 5.2 Who has less income

On every path about 30% of UK households have at least £1 a year less income under the adjusted Triple Lock than under the triple lock in 2039-40: the share is set by who receives a State Pension, while the size of the loss depends on the path (Table 3). On the OBR long-term path those households have £801 a year less on average, against £35 on the OBR central forecast. On the OBR long-term path single pensioners have 0.8% less income than under the triple lock on average, and pensioner couples 1.3%.

Figure 6 ranks households by equivalised household net income before housing costs under the triple lock, with each decile holding a tenth of people, and gives each decile’s total change in income as a share of its total income. Relative to income, the change is largest in the 3rd decile, at 0.56% of income, and smaller in the lowest-income and in the higher-income deciles. The guarantee credit, which makes up a lower pension pound for pound, and the State Pension’s smaller share of higher incomes both work in this direction.

On the OBR central forecast, where the pension gap is small, no poverty measure changes by more than 0.03 percentage points. On the OBR long-term path pensioner poverty after housing costs is 0.70 points higher on the absolute measure and 0.75 points higher on the relative one, and on the simulated path with a large gap up to 1.52 points higher (Table 5). Poverty among everyone changes much less. The absolute line is 60% of the 2024-25 median, held fixed in real terms by uprating it with each path’s CPI; the relative line is 60% of median income in each run, so it is itself a little lower under the adjusted rule. Pension Credit makes up much of a lower pension for pensioners on the guarantee, but not for those just above it.

5.3 Example pensioners

Four hypothetical pensioners, run as households under each rule on the simulated path with a middle-sized gap, show how tax and benefits pass on a lower State Pension (Table 6, Figure 7); the OBR long-term run carries no example households. Each receives the full flat-rate State Pension and, unlike the survey runs, claims every benefit it is entitled to. A lower State Pension means less income tax for a pensioner above the personal allowance and more Pension Credit for one entitled to it; Pension Credit counts income after tax (State Pension Credit Regulations 2002), and council tax reduction disregards the income of a pensioner receiving the guarantee credit (Council Tax Reduction Schemes (Prescribed Requirements) (England) Regulations 2012). The last row of Table 6 gives the share of the lower pension that reaches each pensioner’s income in 2039-40.

Thresholds explain the differences, and two modelling conventions set the thresholds (Section 6). The Pension Credit guarantee rises with earnings under both rules, and the personal allowance stays at £12,570 to 2030-31 and then rises with CPI, so the full new State Pension is above the allowance and taxed in every year. With only the full new State Pension, a pensioner sits close to the guarantee after tax. Under the adjusted rule the pension (a) therefore receives more guarantee credit than under the triple lock in some years (2031-32, 2032-33, 2035-36 and 2039-40), and with it more council tax reduction. In those years more of the lower pension is made up, which is why the line in Figure 7 moves from year to year. The pensioner with a £15,000 private pension (b) is above every threshold and bears most of the loss, less income tax. The council tenant (c) is better off under the adjusted rule in 2039-40: guarantee credit brings maximum Housing Benefit, £1,334 a year more, and full council tax reduction, and together with less income tax these more than make up the lower pension. The older pensioner on the basic State Pension (d) is on the guarantee under both rules: extra guarantee credit makes up the lower pension in full, but they receive £272 less savings credit. Whether (a) and (c) reach the guarantee depends on both conventions: if the guarantee instead rose by the same percentage as the State Pension under each rule, or if the personal allowance were higher, they would be less likely to cross it.

6 Method

6.1 Model and data

Every saving and household figure for a given path is a full run of PolicyEngine UK (PolicyEngine 2026b, 2026c) on the whole survey population, once under each rule. All runs use one release bundle: policyengine 6.11.1, which pins policyengine-uk 2.151.0 and policyengine-core 3.33.3, with the national Microcosm dataset for 2024-25 released with it (build a6299504, 78,234 households) (PolicyEngine 2026a). Constituency and local-authority runs use the same release’s local-area cut. The Enhanced Family Resources Survey, release 1.58.2, runs only for the check against DWP’s caseloads (Table 8). A path’s CPI and earnings growth replace the model’s economic assumptions before the data load, so benefit rates, tax thresholds and earnings follow it, and every run checks that they do. Only the basic and new State Pension differ between the two runs of a path. Under both rules the additional State Pension rises with September CPI and State Pension age follows the law’s timetable. Money is in cash terms for the UK unless stated.

Three conventions shape the net saving. First, the Pension Credit guarantee rises with May–July earnings, the minimum section 150A requires, under both rules. If it instead rose by the same percentage as the State Pension under each rule, it would be lower under the adjusted rule, the Pension Credit offset would be smaller and the net saving larger. Second, the income tax personal allowance stays at £12,570 to 2030-31, as current law sets it, and then rises with each path’s September CPI; the full new State Pension is above it throughout, so a lower pension means less income tax even for a pensioner with no other income. Third, Pension Credit take-up follows the dataset’s own claim decision for each benefit unit, so a household newly entitled under the adjusted rule claims only if that decision says it would; Housing Benefit and council tax reduction respond only for households already receiving them, and passporting from the guarantee credit follows actual receipt.

6.2 Paths of prices and earnings

The simulated paths come from a model of monthly changes in the CPI index (Office for National Statistics 2026d) and in average weekly earnings (Office for National Statistics 2026a), fitted to data from 2000 to the latest published month, with the furlough months of 2020–21 left out. Each month’s change depends on the previous month’s changes in both series, with fixed coefficients; the shocks are past pairs of residuals drawn at random, so prices and earnings keep their joint variation, but the model has no periods of higher or lower volatility and no uncertainty about its coefficients. From the simulated months come both the statutory inputs (September CPI, May–July earnings growth) and the calendar-year growth the rest of the model uses. Every path is then shifted by the same smooth drift so that the average matches the OBR’s forecast in every year, while each path keeps its own ups and downs. The earnings scenarios move that drift by 0.5 points a year from 2031. In the inputs that set the April rises of 2011 to 2026, with April 2022 as in law, the lead between CPI and earnings changed 0.33 times a year.

6.3 The population in each year

The dataset represents households in 2024-25, and each record stands for a person of its age in every year. Through 2025, the year the dataset calibrates to, each record keeps its own weight; after it, household weights are reweighted so that each age and sex group grows as in the ONS’s 2024-based population projection (Office for National Statistics 2026e). Ages are not top-coded, so every recorded age is kept. Each person’s State Pension type follows from their date of birth: those who reached State Pension age before 6 April 2016 are on the basic State Pension, later cohorts on the new one (Pensions Act 2014). Deaths, migration and contribution histories are not simulated, and pensioners abroad are outside the dataset. Records carried forward from today’s pensioners may understate the entitlements of people reaching State Pension age in the 2030s, most of whom will have spent their whole working lives under the new State Pension.

6.4 Testing the model on past forecasts

An average over the simulated paths needs a model whose paths behave like the real inputs, so we registered a test on past OBR forecasts (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026b) before scoring any model. Five versions of the model of prices and earnings are fitted only on data available before each forecast, centred on that forecast, and scored on the gap between earnings and CPI in each year, on how often the lead changes and the floor binds, and on the gap the two rules open after four rises. One test uses seven forecasts, one a year from the March 2016 EFO to the March 2022 EFO; another uses thirteen, from the June 2010 Budget forecast to the March 2022 EFO. Table 7 gives the pass rule.

Under the first rule every version failed, because April 2022 was scored on the published earnings: each had only one of six four-year outcomes inside its band, because the furlough schemes distorted 2021 pay growth. We then changed that one element, before scoring again, to score April 2022 as the law applied it, with the earnings leg suspended; the rest of the rule is unchanged, and the change was made after seeing scores. The second rule also fixed what follows a failure: only the main version may supply an average, no other version may be promoted on its scores, and if the main version fails the release reports scenarios only.

On the Budget’s inputs the main version failed one condition (Table 9). Its average error in the share of years in which earnings growth falls below the floor was 0.267 on the seven-forecast test, against a limit of 0.25: it expected earnings below 2.5% in 44.6% of the years tested, against 17.9% in practice. It passed every other condition: the outcome fell inside its central 80% band in five of seven four-year windows and 83% of annual checks. Two other versions passed and two failed on the same condition, but the rule forbids promoting another version, so the release carries no expected value and this paper reports the scenario envelope only. The test has little power. With seven overlapping four-year windows, a model whose bands cover far less than 80% could still pass, so passing would show only that the model is not clearly wrong; failing one condition by 0.017, less than that statistic’s standard error of 0.085, does not show the model is badly wrong either.

6.5 Future releases

A third registered rule governs releases that use OBR forecasts published after 28 October 2026; this release does not use it. Each such release publishes two averages of the main version’s paths, one with equal weights and one recalibrated so that each year’s share of paths with earnings growth below 2.5% matches the model’s past error on that statistic. Both carry the label model-conditional sensitivity and sit beside the scenario envelope; neither may be called central, expected or a forecast. A prospective test on outcomes nobody has yet seen, from determination year 2027, decides only whether the label may become model-conditional estimate, and it gives no verdict until eight determination years have joined the test, so not before October 2034, when September 2034 CPI appears. On simulated outcomes a correct model passes 35.8% of the time after four years, 68.5% after eight and 85.6% after twelve, and a model that states double the true rate of earnings growth below the floor passes 35.1%, 43.1% and 63.7%: four years cannot tell the two apart.

7 Limitations

No expected value. The four scenarios bound what the rule saves on named paths; they say nothing about how likely each is. The model that would weight the paths failed its registered test, so the paper gives no average over them.

One model of prices and earnings. The simulated paths and the earnings scenarios rest on one model of monthly CPI and earnings; models with periods of higher and lower volatility or uncertain coefficients could give different paths.

No suspensions. The triple lock is applied every year. Parliament suspended its earnings leg once, for April 2022; a future suspension in a year of high earnings growth would lower the triple lock’s cost and so the adjusted rule’s saving.

The OBR’s long-term path. After 2030 the central path follows the OBR’s March 2026 long-term determinants, which the OBR describes as not a forecast. A rebuild on the October 2026 determinants, once published, would replace every figure for 2031 and later.

Population reweighted, not aged. Later pensioners’ amounts come from today’s records of the same age; deaths, migration, contribution histories and pensioners abroad are not modelled. The dataset’s State Pension spending in Great Britain is 98% of DWP’s forecast.

Benefits in the survey runs. Housing Benefit and council tax reduction respond only for households already receiving them, so nobody the rule makes newly entitled starts claiming them, which understates those offsets and overstates the net saving. Pension Credit take-up follows the dataset’s own claim decision, so some newly entitled households do not claim. The dataset’s Pension Credit spending is 5% above DWP’s, which may overstate the Pension Credit offset and understate the net saving. The net direction is unclear.

Tax and the guarantee. The personal allowance stays at £12,570 to 2030-31 and then rises with CPI, and the Pension Credit guarantee rises with earnings under both rules. Other choices for either would change the income tax and Pension Credit offsets.

Rents and other income. Rents and council tax stay at their 2030 amounts after 2030, which understates housing costs, and so poverty after housing costs and the Housing Benefit offset. Dividend, property, savings and self-employment income do not follow the path.

No behavioural response. Nobody changes how much they work, save or claim because of the rule, and indirect taxes are not modelled.

State Pension age addition. PolicyEngine UK has no age addition at 80; both rules share this.

8 Conclusion

The adjusted Triple Lock keeps the triple lock’s protection against prices and its 2.5% floor, but lets the pension fall back to its April 2029 value relative to earnings after years in which prices or the floor beat earnings. Its saving therefore depends on how often the lead passes between prices and earnings, not only on their expected growth. On the OBR’s smooth central forecast it saves £0.5bn in 2039-40; on the OBR’s own long-term assumption about the triple lock it saves £10.9bn; and averaged over simulated paths with earnings growth 0.5 points lower or higher it saves £11.4bn or £11.9bn, £7.8bn or £8.1bn after tax and benefits respond. These are scenarios, not a range with probabilities attached: our model of prices and earnings failed a registered test on past forecasts by overstating how often earnings growth falls below the floor, so the paper gives no expected value. DWP’s £15bn for Great Britain rests on a path it does not publish and is not comparable like for like.

On every path about 30% of households, nearly all with a pensioner, have less income than under the triple lock, with Pension Credit and income tax making up part of the difference; on the OBR long-term path pensioner poverty after housing costs rises by 0.7 percentage points or less. The rule would also require a change to the statutory earnings link. The wider lesson applies to any uprating rule that takes the highest of several indices: its cost depends on how the indices vary from year to year, and costing it on a single smooth path understates the cost, as the OBR’s long-term premium already recognises. Later releases will publish averages over the paths as labelled sensitivities, and a test on new outcomes can say whether they may be called estimates no earlier than October 2034.
