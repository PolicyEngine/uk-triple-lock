Abstract

Pension rules paying the highest of several indices ratchet above earnings; we ask what an earnings-anchored rule removes. From April 2030 the UK’s adjusted Triple Lock raises the State Pension by the highest of 2.5%, CPI and what keeps its April 2029 value relative to earnings. Its excess over its earnings path follows a Lindley recursion, so the gap from the triple lock grows only in earnings-led years after price- or floor-led ones. Replayed from April 2012, it removes 42% of the triple lock’s excess. We run both rules for every household in Microcosm, a calibrated UK household dataset built on the Family Resources Survey, with the PolicyEngine UK tax-benefit microsimulation model. A Bayesian model of the uprating inputs, fitted to the OBR’s forecast errors and earlier outturns, puts the median gross saving in 2039-40 at about £9bn, with a little under half of paths above £10bn, if forecast errors over 2027–38 are as large as over 2011–26; without 2021–23 the median is £6.8bn, and with errors the size of past outturn variation £8.0bn. About £2.8bn of the median comes from how the statutory inputs differ from calendar-year outturns. On the OBR’s central path the saving is £0.5bn.

Keywords: pension indexation; triple lock; microsimulation; Bayesian forecasting; uncertainty; public policy. JEL codes: H55, H68, C11, C53.

1 Introduction

Indexation rules set how pensions in payment keep pace with prices and earnings, and with them much of a pension system’s long-run cost (Vidal-Meliá et al. 2009; OECD 2023). A rule that pays the highest of several indices each year is asymmetric: it passes on every year in which one index leads and keeps that gain in all later years. We ask which part of such a rule’s excess over earnings an earnings-anchored rule removes, on which paths of the indices, and how large the saving is under a forecast of the indices that states its uncertainty.

Since April 2011 the triple lock has raised the State Pension each April by the highest of prices, earnings growth and 2.5% (prices measured by the Retail Prices Index (RPI) in its first year and by the Consumer Prices Index (CPI) since), except in April 2022, when the law suspended the earnings leg (Section 2). Because it takes the highest of three figures every year, a year in which prices or the floor run ahead of earnings raises the pension for good, and later rises build on the higher level. The Office for Budget Responsibility’s (OBR) long-term projections assume that the triple lock raises the pension 0.6 percentage points a year faster than earnings (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026c), and it estimates that by 2029-30 the triple lock will have added £15.5 billion a year to spending relative to earnings uprating since its introduction (Office for Budget Responsibility 2025).

The UK Government announced the adjusted Triple Lock on 29 September 2026, the day on which the Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) published its analysis of the change (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b), and Autumn Budget 2026, delivered on 28 October 2026, confirmed it (HM Treasury 2026). The triple lock continues to apply to each uprating up to and including April 2029; from April 2030, after a year in which price inflation or the floor exceeds earnings growth, the uprating returns the State Pension towards its April 2029 value relative to earnings (Section 3.1). The Budget puts the saving at: “Adjusting the Triple Lock is estimated to reduce State Pension spending by £15 billion a year by the late 2030s, and by £50 billion a year by the late 2040s” (HM Treasury 2026, para. 1.29), citing DWP’s analysis, and it recognises that the saving depends on the path of the indices: “Under the Triple Lock, the cost of the State Pension depends on which of prices, earnings or 2.5% is highest in each year, and on how those differences compound over time. That makes it inherently hard to forecast” (HM Treasury 2026, Box 5.A). We quantify that dependence. The Budget links the saving to funding a new settlement for older people, including personal care in England (HM Treasury 2026, ch. 1), and the OBR notes that the long-term effect of the two announcements together depends on that reform’s cost (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a, para. 3.54); we cost the pension change alone.

The adjusted Triple Lock keeps the triple lock’s protection against prices and the floor but removes the ratchet: the part of the triple lock’s excess over earnings that it keeps after earnings lead again. The adjusted rule still pays above its earnings path while an excess lasts, so its saving is less than the triple lock’s whole premium over earnings uprating. We estimate the adjusted rule’s effect against the triple lock from 2027-28 to 2039-40 with PolicyEngine UK (Ahmadi et al. 2026), a tax-benefit microsimulation model, run on the whole survey population. The runs cover three kinds of path: the OBR’s central path; the same path with the OBR’s long-term triple-lock premium applied to the triple lock only, which we call the OBR long-term path; and simulated paths of prices and earnings. A Bayesian model of the uprating inputs then forecasts the distribution of the saving, and the runs convert its paths to cash.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it gives a closed form for the gap between a max-of-indices rule and its earnings-anchored variant. The anchored rule’s excess over its earnings path follows a Lindley recursion (Lindley 1952), the recursion of a queue’s waiting time: it grows when prices or the floor lead and shrinks, never below zero, when earnings lead. The gap therefore grows each year by the smaller of earnings’ lead and the excess the anchored rule still carries: it depends on the order of the indices’ leads and not only on their average, as a lookback option’s payoff does (Goldman et al. 1979) (Section 3.3). Two corollaries follow: a costing that enters the triple lock’s excess as a smooth premium attributes all of it to the ratchet, and a lead for prices or the floor in the last year saves nothing. Second, it forecasts the saving with its uncertainty, where the official costings give single figures (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b; HM Treasury 2026). The forecast comes from a Bayesian vector autoregression in the deviations of the two inputs from a forecast path (Doan et al. 1984; Litterman 1986), with Student-t shocks, fitted to the OBR’s forecast errors since 2011 and to the statutory outturns before them (Section 4.4). Third, it tests stochastic models of the uprating inputs against official forecast vintages under a registered pass rule, one written into a public, dated analysis plan before the scores it governs (Casey et al. 2012; Olken 2015), and reports the negative result: two registered models failed, and a record of thirteen yearly forecasts tests any such model only weakly. We revised the rule once, after the first scores, and committed the revision before computing any score under it (Appendix C.2). Each part of the plan preceded its code by minutes, so the record shows the order of plan and results, not their independence. We designed the forecast model after both registered models failed and after we had seen a sensitivity conditional on stated volatility (Appendix F); it has passed no test, and Section 4.4 states why we report it.

The main result is the forecast of the saving. If forecast errors over 2027–38 are as large as over 2011–26, the median gross saving in 2039-40 is about £9bn and a little under half of paths save more than £10bn (Section 5.1). Without 2021–23 in the record the median is £6.8bn, and with errors the size of past outturn variation £8.0bn (Section 5.1). The median exceeds the OBR central path’s own saving, £0.5bn, because the forecast’s paths are centred on that path and the saving comes from variation around it. We set four scenarios beside the forecast (Section 5.3). On the OBR’s central and long-term paths the rule removes all of the triple lock’s excess over earnings, because both are smooth paths on which earnings lead in every April after the price- or floor-led ones. The loss falls on households that receive a State Pension and is largest, relative to income, in the lower-middle income deciles (Section 5.7). The evidence that needs no model of the indices’ future is the replay: started in April 2012, the rule removes 42% of the triple lock’s excess (Section 3.3, Section 3.4).

Section 2 describes the triple lock’s record and relates the paper to the literature. Section 3 states the two rules, derives where the saving comes from and replays the rule on past inputs. Section 4 describes the data, the microsimulation, the paths of prices and earnings, the two registered models that failed their test and the Bayesian forecast model. Section 5 gives the forecast of the saving and what it rests on, then the scenarios as checks of robustness, the mechanism along single paths and the distributional effects, setting the forecast and the scenarios against the official costings as it goes. Section 6 concludes, draws out what the results imply for costing such rules and states the main limitations. The appendices give the proofs, details of the model and the data with the reconciliation with DWP’s costing, the record of the two registered models and their test, the checks of the Bayesian forecast, diagnostics of the scenarios, the distribution of the saving under stated volatility and four example pensioners.

2 Background and related literature

2.1 The triple lock since 2011

Figure 1 shows the rises actually paid in each April from 2011 to 2026. RPI set April 2011’s rise, 4.6%, under that year’s up-rating order (Social Security Benefits Up-rating Order 2011); the CPI rule would have given 3.1%. From 2012, CPI set the rise five times, earnings six times and the floor four times. In April 2022 the law suspended the earnings leg, whose input was 8.4% on today’s figures, so that year’s rise was CPI, 3.1% (Social Security (Up-rating of Benefits) Act 2021). Over the sixteen rises the pension rose by 89.4% as paid.

2.2 Related literature

Automatic adjustment mechanisms link a pay-as-you-go pension system’s benefits or contributions to indicators, so that its finances respond to demographic and economic change without new legislation (Vidal-Meliá et al. 2009). The indexation of pensions in payment is one such link: countries index to prices, to earnings or to a mix of the two, and the choice sets how a pensioner’s income moves relative to workers’ incomes over a retirement (Whitehouse 2009; OECD 2023). Portes (2023) places the triple lock in the longer history of the State Pension’s value relative to earnings. These studies compare rules by their cost and adequacy on given paths of prices and earnings; we know of none that derives how the cost of a rule paying the highest of several indices depends on the order in which the indices lead.

Such a rule has the structure of a path-dependent option. One year’s rise is the payoff of an option on the maximum of several assets (Stulz 1982); the triple lock’s excess over earnings compounds a yearly local floor, as a cliquet or ratchet guarantee does (Hardy 2003; Tiong 2000); and the adjusted rule’s gap depends on a running minimum of the cumulative difference between the indices, as a lookback option’s payoff depends on the running minimum of a price (Goldman et al. 1979). Spitzer’s identity gives the expected excess when the yearly differences are independent (Spitzer 1956). We use these identities for the structure of the payoff only. We measure the cost in cash, undiscounted and with no charge for risk, as official costings of uprating rules do, not at the market value that public guarantees carry (Lucas 2012). Nor do we value the risk that indexation shares between generations (Ball and Mankiw 2007; Bohn 2009): the triple lock insures pensioners for good against years in which prices or the floor beat earnings, the adjusted rule only until earnings catch up, and we measure the fiscal side of that transfer.

Stochastic projections of social security finances express the uncertainty of long-run pension spending as distributions over paths of demographic and economic inputs (Lee and Tuljapurkar 1998). The OBR treats the triple lock as a source of fiscal risk (Office for Budget Responsibility 2025); in the long-term scenarios of its 2026 Fiscal risks and sustainability report, as summarised in the October 2026 outlook, the triple lock accounted for around a third of the projected rise in State Pension spending as a share of GDP (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a, para. 3.48). Researchers can test a model that supplies such a distribution against outcomes: probability integral transforms check its calibration (Diebold et al. 1998), and proper scoring rules reward a forecaster for reporting its true predictive distribution (Gneiting and Raftery 2007), including for multivariate outcomes (Scheuerer and Hamill 2015). Bayesian vector autoregressions, which shrink the coefficients of a small system towards a prior, are a standard tool of macroeconomic forecasting (Doan et al. 1984; Litterman 1986; Bańbura et al. 2010; Karlsson 2013), and the data can inform the prior’s tightness (Giannone et al. 2015). Student-t shocks (Geweke 1993) improve such models’ density forecasts (Chiu et al. 2017), and density forecasts are better calibrated when volatility can change over time (Clark 2011), which our forecast allows only through those fat tails. Forecasters also size the uncertainty around a projection from their own past errors (Reifschneider and Tulip 2019). Our forecast joins the two: a Bayesian autoregression fitted to the errors of official forecasts, with priors set by judgment. We know of no earlier test of a stochastic model of uprating inputs against official forecast vintages before a costing uses its paths.

Static tax-benefit microsimulation applies the rules of the tax and benefit system to a representative survey of households, with behaviour held fixed, and is the standard tool for the distributional analysis of benefit reforms (Sutherland and Figari 2013; Richiardi et al. 2021); PolicyEngine UK adds behavioural responses of labour supply and capital gains realisations (PolicyEngine 2026c). Dynamic microsimulation models instead project individual life courses, including pension entitlements, forward in time (Li and O’Donoghue 2013); DWP’s costing of the adjusted Triple Lock uses one (Appendix B.1). The net saving also depends on how many newly entitled pensioners claim Pension Credit, and take-up of means-tested benefits responds to the size of the entitlement and the cost of claiming (Pudney et al. 2006).

3 Policy rules

3.1 The two rules

Under the triple lock the full basic and new State Pension rise each April by the highest of the CPI 12-month rate for the previous September (Office for National Statistics 2026c), growth in average weekly earnings (whole-economy total pay) in May to July of the previous year (Office for National Statistics 2026b), and 2.5%. The first two are the uprating, or statutory, inputs, which ONS publishes to 0.1 point, and the determination year is the year whose inputs set the next April’s rise. We write ctc_t and ete_t for the inputs published before April tt and ff for the floor; fiscal year tt pays the level set in April tt.

The adjusted Triple Lock follows the triple lock to April 2029. From April 2030, the switch, the State Pension rises “by the highest of 2.5%, inflation, or the amount required to maintain its value relative to earnings as reached in April 2029” (HM Treasury 2026, para. 5.23 and Box 5.A). With LtL_t the pension’s level and AtA_t an earnings path that starts at the April 2029 level, the anchor, and grows with May–July earnings, At=At−1(1+et)A_t = A_{t-1}(1 + e_t),

Lt=max⁡(Lt−1(1+max⁡(ct,f)),At).L_t = \max\bigl(L_{t-1}\,(1 + \max(c_t, f)),\; A_t\bigr).

The rules can first differ in April 2031, so the saving in 2030-31 is zero. Two choices are ours: the rule uses the triple lock’s measures, and we round any top-up to the earnings path up to the next 0.1 points. In a year when the rule returns towards its earnings path it rises by less than earnings, so it needs primary legislation: section 150A of the Social Security Administration Act 1992 and section 132A of its Northern Ireland counterpart require the basic and new State Pension and the Pension Credit standard minimum guarantee to rise at least in line with earnings (Social Security Administration Act 1992; Social Security Administration (Northern Ireland) Act 1992).

An April is earnings-led when earnings growth exceeds the higher of CPI and the floor, and price- or floor-led when the higher of CPI and the floor exceeds earnings growth; a change of lead is a change between the two in consecutive Aprils, and a floor year is one in which May–July earnings growth is below 2.5%. The gap is how far the adjusted rule’s level falls below the triple lock’s, in log points (hundredths of a difference in natural logarithms, close to percentages when small). The analysis plan is the public, dated file that specifies each test and model (github.com/PolicyEngine/uk-triple-lock, file docs/METHOD.md).

3.2 Where the saving comes from

When earnings lead, both rules give the same rise. The rules part only after a year in which the higher of CPI and 2.5% beats earnings. Both pay that higher figure, but it lifts the adjusted Triple Lock above its earnings path. When earnings lead again, the triple lock rises with earnings from the higher level, while the adjusted Triple Lock rises only by the higher of CPI and the floor until its earnings path catches up. Under the triple lock each such rise stays in the pension’s level; under the adjusted rule the level returns to its earnings path over the following years.

A two-year example shows the arithmetic. Suppose CPI is 6% and earnings growth 2% in the first year, then CPI 2% and earnings 4%. The triple lock rises by 6% and then 4%, 10.24% in all. The adjusted Triple Lock also rises by 6% in the first year, which leaves it 3.85 log points above its earnings path, and then by the floor, 2.5%, 8.65% in all. In the second year earnings lead by 1.45 log points, less than the excess, so the whole lead becomes the gap: the adjusted rule ends 1.4% below the triple lock (1.59 points less cumulative growth). The next subsection states this in general.

3.4 The rule on past inputs

We can check the decomposition on inputs that have already occurred, with no model of their future path. We call figures computed from the uprating inputs alone, with no microsimulation run, rule arithmetic. The replay uses today’s revised ONS figures, so that both rules see the same inputs. It differs from the rise paid in April 2016, 2019, 2020 and 2025, by 0.1 to 0.3 points, because ONS has since revised the earnings figures, and in April 2011, which RPI set. In April 2022 (Section 2) the replay’s earnings path grows by that year’s CPI, 3.1%, the figure the rise followed, rather than by the published earnings figure. On the replay the triple lock rose 87.4%, against 66.2% for earnings alone and 60.2% for prices alone, which leaves it 12.7% above where an earnings link alone would have taken it (13.9% as paid, Figure 1).

4 Data and method

4.1 Model and data

We compute every saving and household figure for a given path by running PolicyEngine UK (Ahmadi et al. 2026; PolicyEngine 2026b, 2026c) on the whole survey population, once under each rule. Like the static models that Sutherland and Figari (2013) describe, PolicyEngine UK applies the tax and benefit rules of each year to survey households. It can also add behavioural responses; our code switches them off, so behaviour stays fixed in every run (Appendix B).

All runs use policyengine 6.11.1, which pins policyengine-uk 2.151.0 and policyengine-core 3.33.3, with the national Microcosm dataset for 2024-25 published with it (78,234 households, whose records are built on the Family Resources Survey) (PolicyEngine 2026a). We use the Enhanced Family Resources Survey, version 1.58.2, only for the check against DWP’s caseloads (Table 8). A path’s CPI and earnings growth replace the model’s economic assumptions before the data load, so benefit rates, tax thresholds and earnings follow it, and every run checks that they do. Only the basic and new State Pension differ between the two runs of a path. Under both rules the additional State Pension rises with September CPI and State Pension age follows the law’s timetable. We model pension-age Housing Benefit under current law. We give money in cash terms for the UK unless we say otherwise.

The paper’s repository (github.com/PolicyEngine/uk-triple-lock) holds the code, the analysis plan, the stored results (the files of the Bayesian forecast, of the results under stated volatility and of the second model record the random seeds of their draws) and the manuscript, whose build script recomputes every number, table and figure from those files without running a simulation. The replay, the Bayesian forecast, the results under stated volatility and the scores of both registered models are rule arithmetic on public ONS and OBR series and on stored outputs of the microsimulation runs, all of which the repository holds, so a reader can reproduce them without survey data. The microsimulation runs need the Microcosm dataset, to which access is controlled (PolicyEngine 2026a); the repository stores their outputs.

Three conventions shape the net saving. First, the Pension Credit guarantee rises with May–July earnings under both rules. That is the minimum section 150A requires, and neither the Budget nor DWP’s analysis says how the Government will uprate the Pension Credit guarantee under the adjusted Triple Lock, so we take the statutory minimum as the main case; in practice the Pension Credit guarantee has risen by the same percentage as the State Pension in each April since 2022, and by less in earlier Aprils in which prices or the floor set the State Pension’s rise (Appendix B). If it rose by the same percentage as the State Pension under each rule, it would be lower under the adjusted rule, the Pension Credit offset would be smaller, possibly negative, and the net saving larger (Section 5.6 gives a lower bound on the effect).

Second, the income tax personal allowance stays at £12,570 to 2030-31, as current law sets it, and then rises with each path’s September CPI; from April 2031, when the rules can first differ, the full new State Pension is above it, so a lower pension means less income tax even for a pensioner with no other income. The Budget’s Pension Tax Allowance (HM Treasury 2026, Table 6.1) lapses after April 2030, before the rules can first differ, and we assume no successor.

Third, Pension Credit take-up follows the dataset’s own claim decision for each benefit unit, so a household newly entitled under the adjusted rule claims only if that decision says it would; Housing Benefit and council tax reduction respond only for households already receiving them, and passporting from the guarantee credit follows actual receipt.

4.2 The population in each year

The dataset represents households in 2024-25, and each record stands for a person of its age in every year. Microcosm calibrates its household weights once, in 2025, and through that year every run uses the dataset’s own weights. For later years our code rakes those weights so that the weighted number of people in each of 70 age and sex cells grows as in the ONS’s 2024-based population projection (Office for National Statistics 2026e) (Appendix B). The rake targets age and sex only and recalibrates no other total, such as incomes, benefit caseloads or the number of households. The dataset does not top-code ages, so we keep every recorded age.

Each person’s State Pension type follows from the date of birth that their age implies in each year: those who reach State Pension age before 6 April 2016 are on the basic State Pension, later cohorts on the new one (Pensions Act 2014). A record of a given age therefore moves from the basic to the new State Pension as later cohorts reach that age. We do not simulate deaths, migration or contribution histories, and the dataset leaves out pensioners abroad. Records carried forward from today’s pensioners may understate the entitlements of people reaching State Pension age in the 2030s, most of whom will have spent their whole working lives under the new State Pension.

4.3 Paths of prices and earnings

The OBR central path uses the OBR’s October 2026 Economic and fiscal outlook (EFO), published with the Budget, for calendar years to 2030 (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a), and the OBR’s March 2026 long-term economic determinants after that (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026c), which the OBR describes as a projection, not a forecast; the OBR published no long-term determinants with its October forecast. We call the combination the OBR central path. April 2027’s State Pension rise uses the published May–July 2026 earnings growth, 3.9%, and September 2026 CPI, 3.5% (Office for National Statistics 2026b, 2026c). For the Aprils 2028 to 2031 the inputs are the OBR’s September-quarter CPI and April–June earnings growth; later Aprils use its calendar-year figures. The OBR forecasts average earnings on a national-accounts measure, which stands in here for May–July average weekly earnings. Both rules share these inputs. A second single path, the OBR long-term path, keeps these inputs and pays the triple lock at the OBR’s long-term rate (Section 5.3).

The two earnings scenarios of Section 5.3 average the saving over simulated paths. The paths come from a model of monthly changes in the CPI index (Office for National Statistics 2026d) and in average weekly earnings (Office for National Statistics 2026a), fitted to data from 2000 to the latest published month, in which each month’s change depends on the previous month’s changes in both series and the shocks are past pairs of residuals drawn at random; we call it the baseline model. Every path is then shifted by the same smooth drift so that the average calendar-year growth matches the OBR’s forecast in every year, and the earnings scenarios move that drift by 0.5 points a year from 2031. We run a stratified sample of the model’s draws through the microsimulation under both rules. Appendix C.1 gives the model, the scenarios’ statutory inputs and the estimator.

An average over simulated paths needs a model whose paths behave like the real inputs. Before scoring any model we registered a test on past OBR forecasts (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026b), with a pass rule on the coverage of central bands, on biases and on proper scoring rules (Gneiting and Raftery 2007; Scheuerer and Hamill 2015) (Appendix C.2). The baseline model failed one condition: it expected earnings growth below 2.5% in 44.6% of the years of its seven-forecast test, against 17.9% in practice, a floor bias above the rule’s limit. A second model, built on the OBR’s past forecast errors and registered before its scores, met every scored condition but one: its variogram score, a proper score of how the yearly gaps move together, is 1.295 times the baseline model’s, above the limit of 1.25 (Appendix C.3). The test cannot separate either failing figure from its limit, and it has little power. Under the registered rule the paper therefore reports scenarios, and gives the second model’s distribution of the share saved once, in Appendix C.3; Section 4.4 says why the paper nonetheless gives a forecast.

4.4 A Bayesian forecast of the inputs

The forecast needs a distribution of the uprating inputs over the next twelve years. We model how far the two inputs fall from an official forecast path, fit that model to how far they fell from the OBR’s past forecasts, and simulate paths around the OBR central path. The model is Bayesian: each parameter has a prior distribution, the data update it to a posterior, and the forecast, the posterior predictive distribution, averages over the posterior, so its spread reflects uncertainty about the parameters as well as future shocks (Karlsson 2013).

The model describes how September CPI and May–July earnings growth deviate from a forecast path. With εt\varepsilon_t the pair of deviations in year tt, εt=Φεt−1+ut\varepsilon_t = \Phi\,\varepsilon_{t-1} + u_t from ε=0\varepsilon = 0 at the forecast’s origin, where Φ\Phi is a full two-by-two matrix (each input’s persistence, and the pass-through from last year’s prices to pay and from last year’s pay to prices) whose largest eigenvalue in modulus is below 0.98, so deviations die out. The shock utu_t is bivariate Student-t with ν\nu degrees of freedom and scale matrix Σ\Sigma: a normal shock whose variance a single draw each year scales up or down for both inputs together, so that most years are ordinary and a few, like 2021–23, are several times as volatile for prices and pay at once. It is a Bayesian vector autoregression of order one (Litterman 1986) with mean zero at every horizon. Forward, the centre is the OBR central path’s statutory inputs (Section 4.3), and from determination year 2031, where that path passes from the OBR’s forecast to its long-term determinants, each path adds a constant shift to CPI and to real earnings growth that no data here estimate.

Two records inform the parameters, and each year enters once. The first is the OBR’s forecast errors, the statutory input less the OBR’s calendar-year forecast for the same year: for the June 2010 Budget forecast and each March forecast to March 2024, the cells (pairs of a forecast and a horizon) one to four years ahead that the record holds, 55 cells with target years 2011–26; 2026 has one cell, the March 2022 forecast four years ahead, because the record holds no figure for 2026 from the two forecasts after it. An origin’s errors are the process started from zero at the origin, so each cell’s residual, its error less Φ\Phi times the same forecast’s error for the year before (zero at the first horizon), is a draw of utu_t with scale ΣF\Sigma_F. Forecasts made in neighbouring years share target years, so each cell is weighted by one over the number of cells with its target year, and each of the sixteen target years counts once. The weights make this a weighted likelihood, so the posterior is a generalised one (Bissiri et al. 2016) whose spread depends on them; Table 18 gives the forecast with every cell at full weight. The second record is the statutory outturns of 1989 to 2010 around an estimated mean, 21 yearly transitions with scale ΣO\Sigma_O; earnings before 2001 use national-accounts wages and salaries per employee, as in Appendix F. The two records share Φ\Phi and ν\nu; ΣO\Sigma_O’s standard deviations are those of ΣF\Sigma_F times factors κ\kappa, and its correlation differs from ΣF\Sigma_F’s by a shift ζ\zeta on the Fisher scale (the inverse hyperbolic tangent of a correlation, which is unbounded), because variation of outturns around their own mean is not forecast error. The forward paths use ΣF\Sigma_F. Table 2 gives the priors and, for Φ\Phi, ΣF\Sigma_F and ν\nu, which drive those paths, the posterior.

The second model’s registration said that if it failed the paper would report scenarios only. After it failed, we decided that the paper should give a forecast that states its uncertainty, on a Bayesian approach. The specification of this model counts both failures, but it sets no pass rule: the forecast departs from the registered fallback by our decision, not by a test, so the paper’s headline rests on that decision, and both failed verdicts stand. We wrote the specification, with the priors and the checks, before the model’s first fit but after seeing the whole record of forecast errors, every score of the two failed models and the sensitivity of Appendix F, so no hold-out below is clean, and we report the checks whatever they show.

We sample the posterior with the No-U-Turn sampler (Hoffman and Gelman 2014), a Markov chain Monte Carlo method, in NumPyro, four chains of 1,500 warm-up and 2,500 kept iterations. The analysis plan sets one hard gate, on the sampler’s convergence diagnostics (Vehtari et al. 2021), and says that if the gate is unmet after one rerun the model reports no headline. As first coded, with the bound on Φ\Phi as a wall inside the sampler, the model exceeded the gate’s limit on divergent transitions in its first fit and in the rerun. The headline comes from the same posterior sampled without the wall, discarding the draws outside the bound, which meets every limit of the gate. A refit that we specified after seeing the results imposes the bound by a change of variables instead; it meets the gate as written on its first fit and gives a median saving in 2039-40 of £9.27bn, against the headline’s £9.24bn (Appendix D). The divergences came from how we first coded the bound, not from the posterior.

For each of the 9,986 retained draws we draw twenty paths of the inputs for the determination years 2027–38 and their mirror images (the same deviations with their signs reversed), 399,440 paths, and apply both rules to each in rule arithmetic. The cash saving on a path is its share of spending saved times the central run’s basic and new State Pension spending under the triple lock, scaled by the path’s triple-lock level against the central path’s. That identity reproduces the gross saving of each of the five paths we ran through the microsimulation one at a time, the two OBR paths and three simulated paths (Section 5.5; for the OBR long-term path with its stored triple-lock rates, which the rules do not set), and the stratified averages of the earnings scenarios, to within £0.00001bn. The net saving applies the ratio of net to gross saving in those runs, 0.683 (Section 5.1).

Two sets of checks bear on the model, and Appendix D reports both in full. Posterior predictive checks compare the record with records the fitted model generates (Gelman et al. 1996). No registered statistic of the forecast-error record lies outside the central 98% of records replicated from the posterior. These checks are in-sample, and the statistic nearest the band’s edge is the number of changes of lead, the event that drives the saving: the record has more of them than most replications. We also refit the model under two hold-outs, which withhold from each refit the errors that a forecast of that date could not have used or that share its target years, and score its four-year paths exactly as the two registered models were scored. It meets every limit but the one on the floor bias: under one hold-out the floor bias straddles its limit across sets of path seeds, and under the other it exceeds the limit in every set, so the scores cannot show that the model would have passed the test the first two failed.

5.2 What the forecast rests on

The forecast is conditional on the model, its priors and the record, and five features of them move it or bear on how far it can be relied on. First, the record. Its forecast errors from 2021–23 dominate it; Section 5.1 gives the median without them and at the outturns’ scale. Changing one prior at a time, for the persistence, the tails, the correlation and the scales of shocks in both records, and removing or doubling the long-run shift, puts the median between £8.6bn and £9.9bn; quadrupling that shift puts it at £7.2bn (below). Every sensitivity run, listed in Table 18 and Table 19, puts it between £6.7bn and £10.6bn. Among the prior changes, fatter tails move the 95th percentile most, to £31.3bn. Two refits that we specified after seeing every other run lie within that range (Appendix D): the bound on Φ\Phi imposed by a change of variables, which meets the convergence gate as written on its first fit, gives the headline’s median, £9.27bn against £9.24bn, and a fitted, persistent volatility in place of the Student-t shock gives £8.0bn.

Second, the measure. The OBR forecasts calendar-year growth, while the rules use September CPI and May–July earnings, so the record’s errors include how far the statutory inputs differ from calendar-year outturns. Fitting the model to calendar-basis errors lowers the median by £2.5bn, and by £2.8bn against a statutory fit on the same cells, so about that much of the median comes from that difference. Adding it back as a separately estimated term that does not persist from year to year gives £10.6bn, above the headline, so the attribution depends on how that difference is modelled.

Third, the lower tail. The model’s shocks are symmetric, so September CPI falls below zero in 18.8% of forward path-years, against one year in 37 since 1989. Setting every negative input to zero leaves the median at £9.0bn and lowers the 95th percentile (Section 5.1). The upper tail and the mean therefore rest on inputs less plausible than the record, so we report neither the mean nor the largest path saving. Compressing the lower side of every input towards a bound, at zero or just below the record’s lowest input, lowers the median to between £7.0bn and £8.3bn (Appendix D), so up to about £2bn of the headline median rests on shortfalls deeper than a bounded lower tail allows. On the forward paths earnings growth is below 2.5% in 42% of path-years, against 28% of years over 2001–2025, the event that the baseline model overstated when it failed its test (Section 4.3); the centre’s own earnings growth, 2.1% to 3.6% a year, is lower than the record’s. The test’s windows, which cover the inputs of 2017 to 2026, realised a smaller share than either (Section 4.3). Reweighting the forecast’s paths to the share of 2001–2025 puts the median at £9.1bn, and to the share in the test’s windows at £7.9bn; the reweighting also raises the paths’ mean earnings growth, so it is a diagnostic, not a forecast (Appendix D).

Fourth, the long run. The record’s errors stop four years ahead, so years five to twelve rest on the fitted persistence and on priors for a long-run shift that no data here estimate. The record does show persistent level misses: the statutory earnings input fell short of the spring forecasts made from June 2010 to March 2016 by 1.7 points on average three years ahead and 2.2 four years ahead, and exceeded those made from March 2017 to March 2022 by 2.6 four years ahead. A wider long-run shift lowers the median, to £8.6bn with its standard deviations doubled and £7.2bn with them quadrupled.

Fifth, the parameters and the design. The median saving conditional on a single posterior draw of the parameters has a standard deviation of £2.2bn across draws, and the share of paths above £10bn runs from 32% to 62% between the 5th and 95th percentiles of draws. The Monte Carlo error of the median is £0.02bn. We set the priors after seeing the whole record, and the prior on the shock scale is centred near the official one-year errors that the likelihood then fits, so those errors inform the forecast twice. The data move the shock scales and the tails; the posterior medians of each input’s persistence and of the correlation stay close to their prior means (Table 2), so those priors carry through to the forecast. The priors alone, with no data, give a median saving in 2039-40 of £6.9bn, against £9.2bn with the data, a prior predictive check (Gabry et al. 2019). We designed the model after two others failed their tests; it is the seventh model set against the same short record, and we report its checks and apply no pass rule (Section 4.4).

Paying the premium in full from April 2031, in rule arithmetic with caseloads held, raises the gap on the OBR long-term path to 5.51 log points, a share of 5.4%, and the Great Britain saving at DWP’s base to £13.8bn to £14.4bn, so the start date of the premium accounts for most of the residual; Appendix B.1 lists other candidate sources, none of which we test.

The OBR’s own figures measure related but different things. Its replay of 1990–2025, with the anchor set in 1990, puts payments under the adjusted rule 1.4% lower on average than under the triple lock and 4.4% higher than under earnings uprating (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a, para. 3.51), so on that replay the adjusted rule removes about 25% of the triple lock’s average log excess over earnings; the same average on our replay (Section 3.4), over the Aprils from 2012 to 2026, is 1.6%. Its provisional long-term estimate, debt about 25% of GDP lower than under the triple lock and about 10% higher than under earnings uprating in 2075-76 (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a, para. 3.52), corresponds to about 71%. These are averages over years and shares of debt, not of the pension’s level in one year, so they do not compare directly with the shares on our replay and our scenarios.

A further check gives the distribution of the saving if September CPI and May–July earnings growth vary around the OBR central path as they did over 1997–2025, with and without 2021–23 (Appendix F): a sensitivity conditional on stated volatility, on which no test bears. The forecast’s median, £9.2bn, lies between its two medians.

5.4 The OBR central path

The OBR central path is one smooth path. The floor sets the triple lock in April 2029, 2030 and 2031; from April 2032 earnings lead every year (Figure 6). The two rules differ in one year, April 2032: after those Aprils the adjusted Triple Lock rises only by the floor, or by what brings it back to its earnings path, while the triple lock rises with earnings from the higher level. By 2039-40 the full new State Pension is £18,876 a year under the triple lock and £18,840 under the adjusted rule, and the saving is £0.5bn (£0.3bn net). The OBR finds the same for its forecast period: “in our current medium-term forecast an uprating of 2.5 per cent would apply under both the previous and adjusted version of the triple lock in both 2030-31 and 2031-32” (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a, para. 3.50, footnote 17).

5.7 Who has less income

The household results run each path through PolicyEngine UK for the whole survey population, once under each rule. The model recalculates every pensioner’s State Pension and, with it, their income tax, Pension Credit, Housing Benefit, council tax reduction and Winter Fuel Payment. We report them on the OBR long-term path, the larger of the two OBR runs, and give the poverty changes on the OBR central path and the two simulated paths picked by their gap beside it. Cash and percentage changes in income scale roughly with the gap in the pension; poverty rates do not.

On the OBR long-term path households with less income under the adjusted Triple Lock than under the triple lock in 2039-40 have £801 a year less on average, against £35 on the OBR central path. Single pensioners have 0.8% less income than under the triple lock on average, and pensioner couples 1.3%. The share of households with at least £1 a year less income, about 30% on every path, counts the households that receive a State Pension and says nothing about the size of the effect.

6 Conclusion

The adjusted Triple Lock keeps the triple lock’s protection against prices and its 2.5% floor, but lets the pension fall back to its April 2029 value relative to earnings after years in which prices or the floor beat earnings. Its saving therefore depends on how often the lead passes from prices or the floor to earnings, not only on their expected growth. Replayed from April 2012, it would remove 42% of the triple lock’s excess. For the UK in 2039-40, a Bayesian forecast of the uprating inputs puts the median reduction in State Pension spending at about £9bn, with half of paths between £4.0bn and £15.9bn, if forecast errors over 2027–38 are as large as over 2011–26; without 2021–23 in the record the median is £6.8bn, and with errors the size of past outturn variation £8.0bn. After income tax and benefits respond, the median is £6.3bn.

On the OBR’s smooth central path the rule saves only £0.5bn, and the scenarios with earnings growth 0.5 points lower or higher give £11.4bn and £11.9bn, so most of the saving comes from year-to-year variation in the inputs. Scaled to DWP’s population base, our closest scenario leaves 22% to 31% of DWP’s £15bn unexplained, most of which an earlier start of the OBR’s premium would account for. The loss falls on households that receive a State Pension, with Pension Credit and income tax making up part of it, and pensioner poverty after housing costs rises by 0.70 percentage points on the OBR long-term path, a single run whose poverty levels we have not benchmarked. For any rule that takes the highest of several indices, the cost of removing the ratchet depends on how the indices vary: where the lead changes often and price-led years come late, an earnings anchor removes much less of the premium.

The analysis has limits. The forecast’s probabilities hold under its model and priors, which we set after seeing the whole record and two failed models; it departs from the second model’s registered scenarios-only fallback by our decision, not because it passed a test (Section 4.4). As first coded it exceeded its convergence gate’s limit twice; the headline’s fit meets the gate’s limits, and a later refit with the bound imposed by a change of variables meets the gate as written and gives the same median. About £2.8bn of its median comes from how the statutory inputs differ from calendar-year outturns, and beyond four years ahead it rests on fitted persistence and priors. Our runs hold behaviour fixed and leave out indirect taxes, and the forecast converts every path to cash at the central run’s population and entitlements; letting deaths and later cohorts’ entitlements differ, as DWP’s projection does (Appendix B.1), needs new microsimulation runs and is left for future work. Outcomes not yet observed are the only clean test of any of these distributions (Appendix C.2).

C.2 The test on past forecasts

We registered the test on past OBR forecasts (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026b) before scoring any model. The section of the analysis plan that sets the rule used here is dated 6 October 2026. Five versions of the model of prices and earnings enter the test: the baseline model (monthly steps, one lag, resampled residuals) and variants with two lags, annual steps, Student-t shocks and normal shocks. We fit each only on data available before each forecast, centre it on that forecast, and score it with proper scoring rules for each statistic and for the joint outcome (Gneiting and Raftery 2007; Scheuerer and Hamill 2015) and with the coverage of central bands. The scores cover the gap between earnings and CPI in each year, how often the lead changes, how often earnings growth falls below 2.5% and the gap the two rules open after four rises. The test scores changes of lead between earnings and CPI, not the higher of CPI and the floor that the rule uses; the floor-years condition covers the difference. One test uses seven forecasts, one a year from the March 2016 EFO to the March 2022 EFO; another uses thirteen, from the June 2010 Budget forecast to the March 2022 EFO. Table 11 gives the pass rule; we fixed the thresholds together, as a broad screen, before we saw any score, and the record of the rule gives no other basis for any one of them. The accuracy condition compares versions with each other, so the baseline model meets it by construction, and its calibration rests on the coverage and bias conditions.

The rule changed once, after the first scores and before the second, in two elements. It scores April 2022 as the law applied it (Section 2): under the first rule every version failed when we scored April 2022 on the published earnings, which the furlough schemes had distorted, with one of six four-year outcomes inside each band (a seventh window closed only when ONS published September 2026 CPI). And it no longer lets another version replace a baseline model that fails: the first rule would have taken Monthly, two lags, which passes, so it would have authorised an average over its paths. Under the second rule only a passing baseline model supplies an average, and otherwise the analysis reports four scenarios: the OBR central path, the OBR long-term path, the historical replay and the paired paths with earnings growth 0.5 points lower and higher. Every other element, including all thresholds in Table 11, is as first registered. We committed the second rule on its own, before we computed any score under it.

With the data available at the October 2026 Budget, the baseline model failed one condition (Table 12, Table 13). Its error in the share of floor years on the seven-forecast test (Section 4.3), an event that sets dt>0d_t > 0, is 0.267, above the limit of 0.25. The seven windows overlap and cover ten distinct years of inputs, 2017 to 2026; earnings growth was below the floor in two of them (2017 and 2020, setting April 2018 and 2021), against about 4.3 that the model’s forecasts imply when each year takes the average of the windows that cover it. The baseline model passed every other condition. The test has little power. With seven overlapping four-year windows, a model whose bands cover far less than 80% could still pass, and because the upper limit on coverage is 100%, the coverage condition cannot detect bands that are too wide. Suppose the OBR’s long-term assumptions of the time extended the four scored years of each forecast made in 2010 to 2014 by eight more. Those twelve-year windows would overlap, and sixteen years of outturns hold at most one independent window of that length. C.3 The second model

After the baseline model failed, we registered a second model of the inputs before scoring it, together with what the paper would report if it failed: scenarios only, every score, and the model’s distribution of the share of spending saved once, in an appendix, as the output of a failed model. The analysis plan holds the registration in a dated section, pushed before the model’s code and before its scores (Table 17). The registration states its own limits: its hold-out is not chronological, so the pools include later forecasts; we designed the model after seeing every earlier score and the whole record of forecast errors; and it is the sixth version of the model scored against these forecasts. The second model centres each path on the OBR’s forecast and takes its errors from the OBR’s own record: the errors of the thirteen forecasts, one a year, from the June 2010 Budget forecast to the March 2022 EFO, one to four years ahead, measured as September CPI and May–July earnings growth less the OBR’s calendar-year forecast for the same year. Each path takes one past forecast’s errors for its first four years, continues with the four-year-ahead errors of the target years that followed, and adds a draw of the uncertainty in the average error. To score a forecast, the model holds out every forecast that shares a target year with it. It faced the same test as the baseline model, with three added checks on its central bands, and met every scored condition but one (Table 16); the past-years check could not be scored, because no record of forecast errors exists before 2011, and does not count as met. On the thirteen-forecast test its variogram score, a proper score of how the yearly gaps move together (Scheuerer and Hamill 2015), exceeds its limit (Section 4.3, Table 16). Across 40 other sets of random seeds, computed after the verdict, outside the registration, the ratio runs from 1.284 to 1.297, so the failure does not depend on the seeds; they vary only the second model’s draws. It does depend on single forecasts. In a check also computed after the verdict and not registered, leaving the score of the March 2012 EFO, March 2013 EFO or March 2014 EFO out of both models’ averages puts the ratio at 1.230 to 1.244, and over all thirteen single drops it runs from 1.230 to 1.361. Appendix F: Results under stated volatility

We added this analysis after both verdicts and before the Bayesian forecast, and it is not part of either registration; it is now a check of robustness on that forecast. It gives the distribution of the saving conditional on stated assumptions about how the inputs vary: a sensitivity, which no test bears on. Every path is centred on the OBR central path’s statutory inputs in every year. September CPI and May–July earnings growth deviate from that path as they deviated from their own averages over a stated span of years: each deviation follows a first-order autoregression, and the two share jointly normal yearly shocks. The persistence, the shocks’ standard deviations and their correlation come from the statutory inputs over the span; May–July earnings growth starts in 2001, so for the years before we use national-accounts wages and salaries per employee, a different, calendar-year measure; starting both spans in 2001 instead moves the medians by less than £0.3bn. We show two spans together, 1997–2025 and the same years without 2021–23. The second span keeps 2020, when May–July earnings fell 0.9%; leaving it out as well would lower the median further, to £5.6bn. Over the first, the yearly shocks have standard deviations of 2.0 points for CPI and 2.1 for earnings growth, with a correlation of 0.31, and a deviation carries 0.36 and 0.26 of itself into the next year; without 2021–23 the figures are 1.3 and 1.5 points, 0.21, and 0.16 and 0.51. We wrote the method, both spans and the reporting into the analysis plan and pushed it before the first code and the first figures (Table 17). It is not a registration: we chose the spans after seeing the standard deviations of calendar-year CPI and national-accounts earnings over spans starting in 1989, 1993, 1997 and 2011, with and without 2021–23, and both models’ results; neither registered model enters it. Each setting has 400,000 paths, half of them the mirror image of the other half about the OBR central path, and rule arithmetic gives each path’s gap between the rules.

The cash saving on each path uses the identity of Section 4.4, and the net saving the runs’ ratio of net to gross saving. Net figures above the largest gross saving we ran (Section 5.1) extrapolate the ratio: in 2039-40 that covers 11.8% of paths under the first span, including its 95th percentile, and 3.3% under the second.

If prices and earnings vary around the OBR central path as they did in 1997–2025, half of the paths save more than £10.7bn in 2039-40, and 90% save between £1.4bn and £23.8bn (Table 22, Figure 9). If they vary as in those years without 2021–23, the median is £6.6bn and 95% of paths save £18.2bn or less. On 2.0% and 6.6% of paths the two rules pay the same in every April, the paths on which, from the switch, earnings never lead after a price- or floor-led April. The rule arithmetic on the OBR central path itself saves £0.5bn, below the 5th percentile in the first setting: every path has the central path’s mean inputs, so the difference comes from the variation around them, as Section 3.3 describes. The spread widens with the horizon because the gap never closes once opened (Figure 9), and the final year’s distribution is skewed to the right (Figure 10).

The median share under 1997–2025 is below the earnings scenarios’ average because deviations here persist from year to year, so the lead changes less often; with no persistence it would be 4.62%, above both. It is below the second model’s (Table 14) because that model’s inputs vary more (Appendix C.3) than these, whose standard deviations are 2.0 to 2.2 points, and its centre drifts. Variation of outturns around their own average is not forecast error around a forecast. The OBR’s spring forecasts’ errors one to four years ahead are of similar size or larger, with standard deviations of 2.0 to 2.7 points for calendar-year CPI and earnings growth and 2.5 to 3.2 for the statutory inputs, but more closely correlated between prices and earnings, at 0.77 to 0.84 and 0.55 to 0.67. A higher correlation lowers the saving: at 0.8, with the same standard deviations and persistence, the medians are £9.0bn and £5.3bn. Appendix G: Example pensioners

Four hypothetical pensioners, run as households under each rule on the model path near the median gap, show how tax and benefits pass on a lower State Pension (Table 23, Figure 11); the OBR long-term run carries no example households, and the model path carries no probability weight. Each receives the full flat-rate State Pension and, unlike the survey runs, claims every benefit it is entitled to. A lower State Pension means less income tax for a pensioner above the personal allowance and more Pension Credit for one entitled to it; Pension Credit counts income after tax (State Pension Credit Regulations 2002), and council tax reduction disregards the income of a pensioner receiving the guarantee credit (Council Tax Reduction Schemes (Prescribed Requirements) (England) Regulations 2012). The last row of Table 23 gives the share of the lower pension that reaches each pensioner’s income in 2039-40.

Thresholds explain the differences, and two modelling conventions set the thresholds (Section 4.1). The Pension Credit guarantee rises with earnings under both rules, and the personal allowance stays at £12,570 to 2030-31 and then rises with CPI, so the full new State Pension is above the allowance and taxed once the rules can differ. With only the full new State Pension, a pensioner sits close to the Pension Credit guarantee after tax. Under the adjusted rule the pensioner (a) therefore receives more guarantee credit than under the triple lock in some years (2031-32, 2032-33, 2035-36 and 2039-40), and with it more council tax reduction. In those years benefits make up more of the lower pension, so the line in Figure 11 moves from year to year. The pensioner with a £15,000 private pension (b) is above every threshold and bears most of the loss, less income tax. The council tenant (c) is better off under the adjusted rule in 2039-40 because of a cliff: the lower pension takes the tenant onto the guarantee credit, which passports maximum Housing Benefit, £1,334 a year more, and full council tax reduction, and together with less income tax these more than make up the lower pension. The survey-wide results cannot reproduce such a gain, because there Housing Benefit and council tax reduction respond only for households already receiving them (Section 4.1). The older pensioner on the basic State Pension (d) receives the guarantee credit under both rules: extra guarantee credit makes up the lower pension in full, but they receive £272 less savings credit. Whether (a) and (c) reach the guarantee credit depends on the conventions: if the Pension Credit guarantee instead rose by the same percentage as the State Pension under each rule, if the personal allowance were higher or if a successor to the Pension Tax Allowance removed their income tax, they would be less likely to cross it, and (a) and (c) would lose the income tax offset.
