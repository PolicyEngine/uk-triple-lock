Summary

On 29 September 2026 the Prime Minister, Andy Burnham, said he would keep the State Pension triple lock until 2030 and then “adjust” it, and that the pension “will hold its value relative to earnings over time” (BBC News 2026). DWP describes the change: from April 2030 the pension will keep the record high value relative to earnings it reaches under the triple lock, and “In any given year it will go up by at least inflation or 2.5% – and anything more that is needed to retain that value” (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b). We read inflation as September CPI, earnings as May–July average weekly earnings, and the record value as the 2029-30 level, and compare this Burnham plan with the triple lock (current law) to 2039-40 using PolicyEngine UK. Every fiscal and household figure in this paper is a full model run of the whole survey population, or of an example household, under each rule; nothing is scaled or extrapolated from another run.

The expected saving. Averaged over 50,000 simulated paths of prices and earnings centred on the OBR’s forecast, 160 of them run in full, the plan reduces State Pension spending by £11.1bn in 2039-40 (Monte Carlo standard error £0.07bn), or £7.9bn (standard error £0.05bn) once income tax, Pension Credit and other benefits respond. The middle 80% of paths runs from £3.9bn to £18.9bn gross. This is an expected value within one model of prices and earnings, not a forecast probability. It is provisional: the model passes the test we set in advance on past OBR forecasts in a dry run on today’s data (the outcome fell inside its range in four of six final years and 80% of annual checks), and the binding test runs on the 28 October 2026 Budget forecast (Section 2.5).

Why the path matters. The plan keeps the triple lock’s floor and its link to prices, but not its carry-forward of years in which prices or the floor beat earnings. It saves money only after such years: the triple lock keeps that higher rise in the pension for good, while the plan waits for earnings to catch up. On the OBR’s smooth central forecast earnings lead almost every year, so the plan saves only £0.9bn in 2039-40 (£0.6bn net). On bumpier paths each year that prices or the floor beat earnings adds to the saving, and the saving can never fall below zero, so the average over many paths is far above the saving on the average path. DWP costs the plan at £15bn that year, on one path through its dynamic model, for Great Britain (Section 2.7).

Households. In 2039-40 about 27% of UK households, nearly all with a pensioner, have a lower income than under the triple lock. A pensioner whose income after tax is at the Pension Credit guarantee comes out even, because extra Pension Credit and a lower tax bill make up the lower pension. A pensioner with a £15,000 private pension keeps 80% of the difference as lower income.

1 Introduction

The triple lock has raised the basic State Pension each April since 2011, and the new State Pension since it began in 2016, by the highest of CPI inflation, earnings growth and 2.5%. Because it takes the highest of the three each year, a year in which prices or the floor run ahead of earnings raises the pension for good, and later rises build on the higher level. The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) assumes in its long-term projections that the triple lock raises the pension by 0.6 percentage points a year more than earnings (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026c).

The Prime Minister set out a change from April 2030 in his speech to Labour’s conference on 29 September 2026. As the BBC reported it, he said he would “adjust” the triple lock, that the pension would rise every year at least by inflation or 2.5%, and that “it will hold its value relative to earnings over time” (BBC News 2026). DWP’s State Pension uprating analysis, published the same day, says the pension will reach “a record high relative to earnings over the coming three years” and from April 2030 will retain “that record high value relative to earnings”: “In any given year it will go up by at least inflation or 2.5% – and anything more that is needed to retain that value” (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b). DWP does not state the measures of inflation and earnings or the base year. We take the triple lock’s own measures, September CPI and May–July average weekly earnings, and the 2029-30 level as the base, and call the result the Burnham plan.

The paper follows the order of the dashboard. Section 2 gives what the plan saves across the model’s paths, and how that compares with DWP’s and other published costings. Section 3 shows how the triple lock works and what the plan would have paid had it started earlier. Section 4 and Section 5 run both rules on the OBR’s central forecast and on simulated paths. Section 6 and Section 7 follow the whole population and example pensioners on one path. Section 8 describes the method, the inputs, our assumptions and the limitations.

Every fiscal and household figure is a full run of PolicyEngine UK (PolicyEngine 2026a, 2026b). We use the release bundle policyengine 6.2.6, which pins policyengine-uk 2.125.1 and policyengine-core 3.33.0 and is published for the Enhanced Family Resources Survey (FRS) for 2024-25, release 1.58.0 (52,846 household records). The survey population is reweighted to each year (Section 8.3). A path’s CPI and earnings growth enter the model’s economic assumptions before the data load, so benefit rates, CPI-indexed tax thresholds and earnings follow it. The State Pension flat rates are then set from each rule. Weekly amounts, rates and the spread of the pension gap across paths are rule arithmetic and are labelled as such. All money is in cash terms. Fiscal years are named by their first year in figures (“2039” is 2039-40).

2 What the plan saves across the paths

2.1 The expected saving

A single path cannot give the saving to expect. The saving comes from years in which CPI or the floor runs ahead of earnings, and the OBR’s central path has almost none after the switch (Section 4). So we average the saving over the model’s paths of prices and earnings (Section 5), estimated from full runs of both rules on 160 of the 50,000 paths. The result is conditional on that one model of prices and earnings: it is not a forecast probability, and it does not cover the choice of model. We report it because the model passes the test we registered before scoring it, in a dry run on today’s data (Section 2.5); the binding test runs on the 28 October 2026 Budget forecast, and if the model fails it, the update removes the expected saving and says why.

Figure 1 and Table 2 give the expected saving by year with its Monte Carlo standard error. That error measures how precisely the runs pin down the average over all the paths, not the range of outcomes. In 2039-40 the expected saving is £11.14bn (SE £0.07bn) gross and £7.86bn (SE £0.05bn) net. Of the gross saving, extra Pension Credit offsets £0.61bn, lower income tax £2.46bn and extra Housing Benefit £0.15bn. In 2034-35, five rises after the switch, the expected gross saving is £3.21bn (SE £0.22bn). The middle 80% of paths runs from £3.9bn to £18.9bn gross in 2039-40.

By 2039-40 the full new State Pension is £931 a year (£17.91 a week) lower under the plan on average across the paths. The gap runs from £329 a year at the 10th percentile of paths to £1,583 at the 90th (rule arithmetic). Figure 2 shows each full run’s 2039-40 saving against its weekly gap. The gross saving is £0.619bn for each £1 a week of gap in every run, because it comes only from the flat rates. The net saving scatters around a lower line, because the benefits that respond have thresholds.

2.2 Single scenarios

Beside the average we show a few named scenarios, each a full run of both rules (Table 1). The OBR’s central forecast and the OBR’s long-term path, on which the triple lock rises 0.6 points a year faster than earnings (Section 2.8), are single runs with no Monte Carlo error. The two earnings scenarios use the same simulated paths as the expected saving, with calendar-year earnings growth moved 0.5 percentage points a year below or above from 2031, and are averages with a Monte Carlo error. Without the shift those paths average £11.1bn; lower earnings growth changes the 2039-40 saving by −£0.7bn and higher by −£0.1bn. The higher-earnings change is within its Monte Carlo error, so the two scenarios are not mirror images. The scenarios are a range of cases, not a probability interval.

2.3 Strata and estimator

The 50,000 paths are not all run. Paths on which the two rules pay the same rise every year save exactly nothing, since both runs get identical inputs. They are 0.79% of paths and form their own stratum. One extra path from it is run to confirm that it saves nothing. The rest are split into 11 strata, each holding an equal share of the paths, by the gap in the full new State Pension in 2039-40. The rule arithmetic gives that gap for every path before any model run. The design has 160 sample slots. They are allocated across the strata in proportion to each stratum’s share of paths times the spread of the gap within it (Neyman 1934), with at least two in every stratum. Two of them sample the stratum of identical rules, so that its weight stays in the estimate if a mean-path scenario makes its saving nonzero. Slots are drawn at random with replacement within each stratum. A path drawn twice is run once and counted twice. The slots give 160 distinct paths, each a full run (Table 3). The spread of the gap stands in for the spread of the fiscal saving, which is not known before the runs.

For any output yy the estimate is μ̂=∑hWhy‾h\hat{\mu} = \sum_h W_h \bar{y}_h, where WhW_h is stratum hh’s share of the paths and y‾h\bar{y}_h the mean over its slots. Its Monte Carlo variance has two parts, and we report them separately. The first is path sampling, ∑hWh2sh2/nh\sum_h W_h^2 s_h^2 / n_h, with shs_h the standard deviation and nhn_h the number of slots in the stratum. The second is the first phase, ∑hWh[sh2+(y‾h−μ̂)2]/N\sum_h W_h [s_h^2 + (\bar{y}_h - \hat{\mu})^2] / N, where NN is the number of simulated paths. It measures how far the simulated paths themselves could fall from the model’s full distribution. The stratum of identical rules counts in the second sum. The standard error we quote is the square root of the two together. Neither part measures the choice of model, which the single scenarios show instead. Tests in the repository check that the estimator is unbiased, that its interval covers about 95% of the time on synthetic cases, and that the published estimates recompute from the per-path records in the results file.

2.4 Choosing the calibration

The paths could be calibrated in more than one way. Besides matching the OBR’s means, they could also be tilted, by entropy tilting (Robertson, Tallman, and Whiteman 2005), so that the variance of the gap between May–July earnings and September CPI, and how often the lead passes between them, match history. We chose between these on two tests of the expected gap between the two rules, not of the saving, since the gap is what the calibration changes.

The first is a chronological backtest over 12 OBR forecasts in the OBR’s historical official forecasts database (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026b), from the June 2010 Budget forecast to the March 2021 EFO. At each, the monthly model is fitted only on data to the December before the forecast, and the calibrations take the forecast’s calendar-year CPI and earnings as the means; the plan starts at the April rise two years after the forecast, and the gap is measured after four rises. This backtest uses today’s published data and is not rerun on the Budget-day inputs. Table 4 compares each method’s expected gap with what happened, scoring April 2022 both as in law (earnings suspended) and with the published earnings. Of the calibrations that match the OBR’s means, which the expected value needs, the drift shift and entropy tilting to the means have almost the same bias: +0.20 ± 0.45 and +0.21 ± 0.45 points of the pension as in law, each within one standard error of zero, and −0.60 ± 0.57 and −0.60 ± 0.57 as published, about one standard error. We use the shift because tilting keeps so few effective paths. Tilting further, to past dynamics, made the bias larger under both treatments (−0.35 ± 0.41 and −1.15 ± 0.52), though its mean absolute error as in law is lower; we chose on bias because the expected value is a mean. The uncalibrated model has the smallest bias as in law (−0.01 ± 0.41) but does not match the OBR’s forecast. The OBR forecast used as a single path has the largest bias under both (−0.86 ± 0.41 and −1.67 ± 0.54): it expects too few changes of lead. The origins overlap, sharing three of four target years with their neighbours, so the standard errors, computed as if they were independent, understate the uncertainty; the data are today’s revised figures, and the model design was chosen with the whole sample in view.

The second, the past-years check of Section 3, cannot test the drift shift, since no forecast covers those years; it bears only on tilting to past dynamics, which put what happened at the 93rd percentile of its paths, against the 56th for the model alone.

The tilts are kept as sensitivities, computed by reweighting the same full runs (Table 5). Across the 12 of them the expected gross saving in 2039-40 runs from £5.59bn to £12.41bn, against £11.14bn for the primary calibration. Reweighting leaves few effective runs in some cases, as few as 43. Those rows’ standard errors are approximate and too narrow. A reweighting with fewer than 100 effective runs keeps its row but is left out of any range we quote. The tilts did worse in the backtest, so this rule promotes none of them.

2.5 The screen set before scoring

An expected value needs a model of prices and earnings whose paths behave like the statutory inputs. We wrote a screen for this and committed it before scoring any model. We call it C1. It scores five forms, fixed in advance. The primary is the monthly model of Section 5, with one lag and bootstrapped shocks. The others are the same model with two lags, the same model with Student-t shocks joined by a t-copula, the same model with Gaussian shocks, and an annual model of calendar CPI and earnings that adds blocks of past gaps between the statutory and calendar measures. Every form is shifted to the same OBR means. Each is fitted only on data from before each forecast origin and scored on what followed.

There are two tests. Test A uses the six spring forecasts from 2016 to 2021 that have two complete earlier blocks of forecast errors. Test B uses all twelve complete spring forecasts from 2010 to 2021. Each test scores four things over the four years after the origin: the gap between earnings and CPI in each year, the number of changes of lead, the share of years with earnings below the floor, and the gap the two rules open after four rises. A form passes only if it meets every condition. Its central 80% bands must cover between half and all of the outcomes, for the yearly gaps and for the final gap, and the band for the final gap must be consistent with 80%. Its biases must be small: at most 1.5 percentage points on the gap, one change of lead per path and a quarter on the floor share. Each of its proper scores must be no more than 1.25 times the primary’s, so that a form cannot pass by spreading its paths wider. A version fitted on data before 2011, with no calibration, must put the gap that actually opened over the rises of 2012 to 2026 between the 5th and 95th percentiles of its paths. Every path and score must be finite. C1 required all this under both treatments of April 2022: with the published earnings, and with the earnings leg suspended as the law applied it. The appendix gives the scores (Section 9).

No form passed C1. All five failed test A with the published earnings. The band for the final gap covered what happened at only one of six origins. The cause is the furlough base effect: earnings growth in May to July 2021 was measured against pay that furlough had held down a year earlier. For the primary this was the only failure. The other forms had further failures. Under C1 as written, no model was adequate and no expected value was allowed.

We then changed the rule, after seeing these scores, and we say so here. The analysis models the statutory rule, and Parliament suspended the earnings leg for April 2022 (“Social Security (Up-rating of Benefits) Act” 2021). The new rule, C2, scores April 2022 as the law applied it. It was written and committed on its own, at commit 65343e2 of the repository, before any code for it was written and before any C2 score. That commit also records the C1 failure. C2 changes two things. First, in the year that set April 2022’s rise, earnings are set equal to CPI, in both the simulated paths and the outcomes. Second, any statistic that this legal rule fixes in both is left out of the scores. Cells are chosen by the legal rule, never by their values. In test A this removes four of the 24 yearly gap cells, the 2021 gap in each window that contains it. The final gap keeps the suspended year in its arithmetic. Every C1 threshold stays as it was, now applied to the suspended treatment in both tests. Scores with the published earnings are reported beside every C2 result, but they have no say in the verdict. C2 does not turn the C1 failure into a pass, and both records stay in the repository.

The binding C2 score uses the inputs available after the Budget: September 2026 CPI, May to July 2026 earnings, and the OBR’s forecast of 28 October 2026. A score on earlier inputs is a dry run and allows nothing. If the original primary passes the binding score, the release keeps a model-conditional expected value beside the single scenarios. If it fails, the build drops the expected value automatically and keeps the scenarios alone, with the failure recorded. No other form is promoted in its place, and forms are never averaged. In a dry run on today’s inputs the original primary passes C2: in test A the outcome fell inside its central 80% band in four of six final years and 80% of the 20 annual checks. This paper reports that provisional result; the binding score follows the 28 October 2026 Budget.

2.6 The survey data against DWP

The gross saving scales with State Pension spending in the data. Every run checks that each flat-rate pension moves exactly in proportion to the flat rate. So the closer the data’s State Pension bill is to DWP’s, the more the gross saving can be trusted. Table 6 sets the Enhanced FRS against DWP’s Spring Forecast 2026 (Department for Work and Pensions 2026a) for 2026-27. DWP’s tables cover Great Britain and include pensions paid abroad. We take the paid-abroad amounts out of DWP’s totals where the tables separate them, and count only households in England, Scotland and Wales in the model, so the two match. No DWP figure exists after 2030-31.

The survey’s State Pension spending is 15% below DWP’s, so the gross saving, which scales with it, is if anything on the low side. Pension Credit is 17% above DWP’s and pension-age Housing Benefit 3% above, so the benefit offsets are if anything on the high side, and the net saving correspondingly a little low.

2.7 DWP’s costing

DWP costs the plan at £15bn in 2039-40 in cash terms (£11bn in 2025-26 prices) (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b). Our expected gross saving is £11.1bn for the UK. Our figures differ from DWP’s for four reasons.

The first is the path. DWP applies one uprating path, which it does not state. Ours are a set of paths calibrated to the OBR’s forecast. On the central path alone we find £0.86bn. For a single path to save £15bn it needs several years in which CPI or the floor beats earnings. Our 90th-percentile path saves £18.46bn gross.

The second is the population. DWP’s model, Pensim3, projects the population and each person’s contribution record. We reweight the survey instead (Section 8.3). Each survey record stands for a person of its age in every year. The weights follow the ONS projection of the population by age and sex, and each person’s State Pension type follows from their date of birth. So later pensioners join on the new State Pension. But their amounts come from today’s records of the same age, and deaths, migration and contribution histories are not simulated.

The third is coverage. The survey’s State Pension spending is 15% below DWP’s matched figure (Table 6), and the gross saving scales with it.

The fourth is scope. DWP’s figure is direct spending for Great Britain, with no tax effects, rounded to the nearest £1bn. It compares with our gross figure for Great Britain, which the results give beside the UK one. DWP also cautions that “These savings should not be used to then derive further point estimates for savings in an individual year and should instead be relied on for long-term estimates” (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b).

2.8 The OBR’s long-term premium

The OBR’s long-term projections assume the triple lock raises the pension 0.6 percentage points a year faster than earnings (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026c). On our paths the triple lock rises 0.55 points a year faster than May–July earnings on average over the April rises of 2034 to 2039; the Burnham plan rises 0.08 points a year slower. Across the calibrations that match the OBR’s means the triple lock’s premium runs from 0.43 to 0.60 points. This is rule arithmetic on the inputs, not a fiscal figure, and it compares the triple lock’s rise with the statutory earnings measure, where the OBR’s premium is over its own measure of average earnings.

2.9 Other published costings

Table 7 sets our closest figures beside other published costings. Only DWP’s costing is of this plan; the others compare the triple lock with earnings uprating, over the years since 2012 or over a long horizon, so they are context, not checks. The OBR estimated in July 2025 that the triple lock would cost £15.5 billion a year by 2029-30 against earnings uprating since 2012 (Office for Budget Responsibility 2025). The Resolution Foundation cites the OBR’s estimate that State Pension spending would be £12.6bn lower this year with earnings-linked uprating than with the triple lock, and puts the net saving at about £9bn after income tax and means-tested benefits (Curtice and Clegg 2026). Had the Burnham plan, which keeps a floor of the higher of CPI and 2.5%, started in April 2012, 2013, 2014 or 2015, we find it would save £7.25bn gross and £5.24bn net in 2026-27. The OBR’s July 2026 report looks further ahead: state pension spending is “projected to rise from 5 per cent of GDP to around 9 per cent of GDP over the projection period in the baseline”, driven by ageing and triple-lock uprating, and with earnings uprating instead it “reaches around 7 per cent of GDP” (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026d). The closest methodological precedent is the Institute for Fiscal Studies’ simulation of the triple lock’s cost from historical inflation and earnings (Cribb, Emmerson, and Karjalainen 2023).

3 How the triple lock works

3.1 The rule and its inputs

Each April the full basic and new State Pension rise by the highest of three figures: the CPI 12-month rate for the previous September (Office for National Statistics 2026d); growth in average weekly earnings (AWE), whole-economy total pay, in May to July of the previous year over the same months a year earlier (Office for National Statistics 2026c); and 2.5%. The law requires the basic and new State Pension to rise at least in line with earnings (“Social Security Administration Act” 1992); the prices leg and the floor are policy on top. ONS publishes both inputs to 0.1 point, and the rules here take them at that precision, so the triple lock’s rate is exactly the larger input or the floor. Only the flat-rate pensions are triple-locked. The additional State Pension rises with September CPI under both rules here, as DWP’s analysis assumes for the parts of the pre-2016 pension that the triple lock does not cover (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b).

3.2 The triple lock since 2011

Figure 3 and Table 8 replay the rule on the published inputs for each April from 2011 to 2026, using the latest ONS figures. CPI set the rise six times, earnings six times and the floor four times. Over those 16 rises the triple lock raised the pension by 88% in all, against 66% for earnings alone, 60% for prices alone and 48% for 2.5% a year. Carrying those higher rises forward leaves the pension 12.7% above where an earnings link alone would have taken it.

In April 2022 the law suspended the earnings leg, whose May–July input was 8.4%, so that April’s rise was CPI (3.1%) (“Social Security (Up-rating of Benefits) Act” 2021); the replay does the same, though the earnings-alone line uses the published figure. The replay differs from the rise actually paid in 6 of 16 years (April 2011: paid 4.6%, rule 3.1%; April 2016: paid 2.9%, rule 2.8%; April 2019: paid 2.6%, rule 2.7%; April 2020: paid 3.9%, rule 4.0%; April 2025: paid 4.1%, rule 4.4%; and April 2026: paid 4.8%, rule 4.9%), because the rises paid used the figures first published, and April 2011’s rise, set by that year’s up-rating order, was larger than the rule gives (“Social Security Benefits Up-rating Order” 2011).

3.3 The Burnham plan’s rule

The Burnham plan follows the triple lock to April 2029. DWP’s description is that from then on “In any given year it will go up by at least inflation or 2.5% – and anything more that is needed to retain that value”, where the value is the pension’s record high relative to earnings (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b). We write this as follows. From April 2030 let LtL_t be the pension’s level and AtA_t an earnings path that starts at the 2029-30 level and grows with May–July earnings, At=At−1(1+et−1)A_t = A_{t-1}(1 + e_{t-1}). Then

Lt=max(Lt−1(1+max(ct−1,f)),At),L_t = \max\bigl(L_{t-1}\,(1 + \max(c_{t-1}, f)),\; A_t\bigr),

where cc is September CPI, ee is May–July earnings growth and ff is the 2.5% floor. The pension never rises by less than the higher of CPI and 2.5%, and never falls below its earnings path. The top-up to the earnings path is rounded up to 0.1 point, so rounding never leaves the pension below it. Property tests in the repository check, for all inputs, that the plan meets both guarantees, that its rate is the smallest that does, and that before rounding its level never exceeds the triple lock’s.

Two features follow from the rule. In its first year, April 2030, the plan always pays what the triple lock pays: its earnings path starts from the 2029-30 level, so the top-up is exactly the earnings rise whenever earnings lead. After that the plan pays less only after some earlier year in which the higher of CPI and 2.5% ran ahead of earnings. Both rules pay that higher figure then, but it lifts the plan above its earnings path; when earnings next lead, the plan rises only by the higher of CPI and 2.5% until earnings catch up, while the triple lock rises with earnings from the higher level.

3.4 If the plan had started earlier

Figure 4 applies the plan’s rule to the published inputs from an earlier April. Started in April 2012, 2013, 2014 or 2015, it would have paid £226.11 a week in full new State Pension in 2026-27, against the £241.30 actually paid: 6.3% less. It pays less than the triple lock in April 2016, 2019, 2020, 2024, 2025 and 2026: each time the plan is still above its earnings path after earlier years in which CPI or the floor ran ahead of earnings. The largest shortfalls follow April 2023, when September CPI of 10.1% set a rise far above earnings growth of 5.5%. Started in April 2024, 2025 or 2026, the plan would have paid the same as the triple lock every year to April 2026. The plan’s earnings path here also grows by CPI in April 2022, as if the suspension of the earnings link applied to it too; with the published figure its earnings path would have been higher from then on, and the shortfalls here smaller.

For 2024-25 to 2026-27, the years the survey data cover, the savings are full model runs in which every flat-rate pension is set to the plan’s level instead of the triple lock’s. Had the plan started in April 2012, 2013, 2014 or 2015, State Pension spending in 2026-27 would have been £7.25bn lower, and the government would have been £5.24bn better off once income tax and benefits respond.

The same comparison tests the monthly model behind our paths, before any shift. Fitted only on data to December 2010, and with no calibration because no forecast covers those years, the monthly model of Section 5 gives a plan started in April 2012 an average gap of 6.1% of the pension by April 2026. What happened gives 6.3%, at the 56th percentile of the model’s paths. This check is one part of the screen in Section 2.5, which scores it with April 2022 as the law applied it.

4 The OBR’s central forecast

4.1 The central path and its sources

The central path uses the OBR’s March 2026 Economic and fiscal outlook (EFO) for calendar-year CPI and average earnings growth in 2027–2030 (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a), and its long-term economic determinants for 2031–2039, converted from fiscal to calendar years (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026c). After 2030 the OBR describes the determinants as a long-term projection, not a forecast. In the determinants calendar-year CPI settles at 2.0% and earnings growth rises to 3.7% by 2039.

The inputs to each April’s rise are taken as close to the statutory timing as the forecasts allow (Table 9). April 2027’s rise uses the published May–July 2026 earnings growth, 3.9%, and August 2026 CPI, 3.1%, standing in for September’s, which ONS publishes on 21 October 2026. CPI would have to rise by more than 0.8 points from August to September to overtake earnings growth; its largest August-to-September rise since 1997 is 0.7 points. The rises of April 2028, 2029, 2030 and 2031 use the EFO’s September-quarter CPI and April–June earnings growth, and later rises the calendar-year path, for which no quarterly forecast exists.

4.2 The two rules on the central path

Figure 5 shows both rules on this path. Earnings grow by less than 2.5% for several years, so the floor sets the triple lock in April 2028, 2029, 2030 and 2031. From April 2032 earnings lead every year. The two rules differ in two years: April 2032 (triple lock 2.7%, set by earnings; plan 2.5%, set by the 2.5% floor) and April 2033 (triple lock 3.0%, set by earnings; plan 2.8%, rising to its earnings path). By 2039-40 the full new State Pension is £359.85 a week under the triple lock and £358.45 under the plan, 0.4% lower.

4.3 Why the saving is small on this path

The whole difference on the central path comes from the floor years just after the switch, April 2030 and 2031. The plan pays the floor then too, but earnings grow by less, so the plan’s pension ends up above its earnings path. When earnings pick up, the plan rises by only the floor, and then by what brings it back to its earnings path, while the triple lock rises with earnings from the higher level. Once the plan is back on its earnings path both rules rise with earnings, and the gap stays where the floor years left it. The plan saves £0.66bn in State Pension spending in 2034-35 and £0.86bn in 2039-40; net of income tax and benefits, £0.46bn and £0.62bn (Table 9).

On a single path like this the triple lock rises with earnings in the long run: the carry-forward pays out only when the lead passes between earnings and the higher of CPI and 2.5%. The central path has no such reversals after 2032. The OBR’s long-term projections instead assume a triple-lock premium of 0.6 points a year over earnings (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026c); Section 2.8 compares that with the premium across our paths.

5 Another path

5.1 A random path

The central forecast is one path of many. Figure 6 runs both rules on a path drawn at random from the 50,000 paths behind the expected saving of Section 2: draw 8,580 of 50,000, picked at random (seed 20261001); its 2039-40 gap is £1.96 a week, at the 2nd percentile of those paths: 98% of them open a bigger gap by then. We keep it because it was drawn at random. The middle and 90th-percentile paths below give the context.

On this path the figure that sets the triple lock changes four times from April 2030, and the rules differ in two years: April 2033 (triple lock 4.0%, earnings; plan 3.6%, rising to its earnings path) and April 2034 (triple lock 9.9%, earnings; plan 9.8%, rising to its earnings path). In April 2032 CPI runs ahead of earnings and both rules pay it; that lifts the plan above its earnings path, and the next year, when earnings lead, the plan rises only as far as its earnings path. In April 2034 the plan’s rate is 0.1 point below the triple lock’s although both follow earnings: the year before, the plan’s rise to its earnings path was rounded up, leaving it just above the path, so it needed slightly less than the earnings rise. By 2039-40 the full new State Pension is £411.59 a week under the triple lock and £409.63 under the plan.

5.2 How the monthly model makes the paths

The paths come from a model of the months themselves, since the triple lock’s inputs are measured over different months from the calendar-year figures the OBR forecasts. It is a vector autoregression on the monthly log changes in the CPI index (Office for National Statistics 2026e) and in the whole-economy AWE total pay level (Office for National Statistics 2026b), with an intercept and month dummies, fitted by least squares from 2000 to the latest published month. The lag order is chosen by the Bayesian information criterion (it picks one). The furlough months of 2020–21 are left out of the fit and of the pool of shocks, because their earnings changes reflect who was paid rather than what pay did. Each simulated month draws a pair of fitted residuals at random, so the shocks to prices and earnings keep their joint distribution; the one month for which CPI is published but AWE is not draws its earnings shock from its normal distribution given the observed CPI shock. From the simulated months come both the statutory inputs (September CPI, May–July earnings growth) and the calendar-year growth that the rest of the model uses, so the two stay consistent on every path. Calendar-year AWE total pay stands in for the OBR’s measure of average earnings.

The model’s own averages sit well away from the OBR’s forecast, so every path is shifted by the same monthly drift: the smoothest drift that makes the paths’ mean calendar-year CPI and earnings growth equal the central path in every year from 2027 to 2039. It starts in September 2026, the first month for which neither series is published. The drift is not small: it moves earnings growth by −1.51 points a year in 2027 and CPI growth by −0.60 points a year by 2039. Because every path moves by the same amount, each path’s ups and downs, and the link between prices and earnings, remain the model’s own. Tilting the weights of the paths to the same means instead (Robertson, Tallman, and Whiteman 2005) would leave far fewer effective paths, and a drift held constant within each year would oscillate between years and put false reversals into the statutory inputs.

The same model with Student-t or Gaussian shocks, the same model with two lags, and an annual model are scored beside it in the screen of Section 2.5. They are not run in full. The screen keeps this model as the primary if it passes, and never promotes another form in its place.

6 The whole population

The same random path runs through PolicyEngine UK for the whole survey population, once under each rule. Every pensioner’s State Pension is recalculated, and with it their income tax, Pension Credit, Housing Benefit, council tax reduction and Winter Fuel Payment. The gross saving is the fall in spending on the basic and new State Pension; the net saving is the change in the government’s balance once taxes and benefits respond. Table 10 gives the same measures on the middle and 90th-percentile paths.

6.1 Gross and net saving by year

On the random path the plan first saves money in 2033-34, £0.66bn gross, and by 2039-40 it saves £1.21bn gross and £0.86bn net (Figure 8, Table 11).

6.2 From gross to net

Table 12 follows the gross saving to the net saving. In 2039-40 extra Pension Credit takes £0.07bn of the £1.21bn gross saving back, lower income tax £0.27bn and the other offsets £0.01bn, leaving £0.86bn: 71% of the gross.

Several features of the runs move the offsets, in both directions. In the survey runs Housing Benefit and council tax reduction respond only for households already receiving them. Nobody the plan makes newly entitled starts claiming, which makes their offsets smaller and the net saving larger. policyengine-uk lets a pension-age family make a new Housing Benefit claim, but it takes up either benefit only for a family that reports it, because the survey always has some family reporting a benefit. Pension Credit take-up is the dataset’s own draw. A lower pension can make a household entitled to the guarantee credit. In the model it then gets the Housing Benefit passport only if it actually receives the guarantee credit, as in law (policyengine-uk #1927, in release 2.123.6). Earlier releases gave the passport on entitlement alone. Rents and council tax stay at their 2030 amounts after 2030, which lowers the most either benefit can pay and probably shrinks both offsets too. The survey has more Pension Credit and pension-age Housing Benefit than DWP forecasts (Section 2.6), which leans the other way: it makes the offsets larger and the net saving smaller.

6.3 Who has lower income

In 2039-40, 27.0% of households have a lower income under the plan on this path (by more than £1 a year), by £94 a year on average. On the middle path the share is 27.1% and the mean loss is £822. By income quintile (Table 13) the mean loss is largest in the 2nd and 3rd quintiles and smallest in the 5th; relative to income the 1st quintile sees the largest fall in income, 0.069% of its income.

Table 14 gives poverty after housing costs. The absolute line is fixed in real terms. The relative line is 60% of median household income in the same run, so when pensions fall the line can fall too. PolicyEngine weights that median by household, where the official statistics weight it by person.

7 Example pensioners

The same random path runs four example pensioners through PolicyEngine UK as households, once under each rule (Figure 9, Table 15). Each receives the full flat-rate State Pension, claims everything it is entitled to, and is the stated age in every year. The examples describe a pensioner of that age in each year, not one person ageing. Their private pensions, rents and council tax are set in 2026-27 terms and grow with the path’s CPI. The renters make a new Housing Benefit claim, as the law allows once every adult in the family is over State Pension age. They report no existing claim, and policyengine-uk lets a pension-age family make a new claim. The examples are hypothetical households, not survey records. On the middle path the difference in State Pension in 2039-40 is £867 a year, against £102 on this one.

A lower State Pension means less income tax for a pensioner above the personal allowance. It means more Pension Credit for one entitled to it, and more council tax reduction or Housing Benefit for one receiving them. It can also bring a pensioner back under the Winter Fuel Payment’s income threshold. In 2039-40 a pensioner on the new State Pension receives £101.72 a year less State Pension under the plan on this path.

The pensioner with a private pension (pensioner (b), single pensioner aged 70 with a £15,000 private pension, owns their home) pays £20.34 less income tax and has the rest as lower income, 80% of the loss.

The pensioner with the State Pension only (pensioner (a), single pensioner aged 70, State Pension only, owns their home) has an income after tax below the Pension Credit guarantee in 2039-40. So Pension Credit rises by £81.37. That is exactly the fall in the pension after the £20.34 fall in income tax. Pension Credit counts income after tax, both in the regulations (“State Pension Credit Regulations” 2002, regulation 17(10)) and in policyengine-uk. The two together therefore make up the whole difference, and the pensioner comes out even. The council renter on the same pension (pensioner (c)) comes out the same way. In 2033-34, 2034-35 and 2035-36 their income after tax is above the guarantee or only just below it, so both have lower income for part of the difference (Figure 9).

Council tax reduction no longer moves for these two pensioners. The regulations disregard the whole income of a person receiving the guarantee credit (“Council Tax Reduction Schemes (Prescribed Requirements) (England) Regulations” 2012, Schedule 1, paragraph 13). policyengine-uk has done the same since release 2.104.2, so their council tax reduction is the same under both rules.

The pensioner on the old basic State Pension (pensioner (d), single pensioner aged 90 on the old basic State Pension, rents from the council at £120 a week) has a smaller difference in State Pension, £77.94. Pension Credit and income tax replace all of it in 2039-40. In 2037-38, 2038-39 and 2039-40 this pensioner does have lower income for part of the difference. Its Pension Credit then includes savings credit, which pays 60% of income above a threshold. A lower pension therefore also lowers the savings credit, and Pension Credit rises by only 40% of the after-tax loss.

The renters’ Housing Benefit does not change in any year on this path: it already covers the full rent, so it cannot rise.

In policyengine-uk the Winter Fuel Payment is paid, outside Scotland, to pensioners on Pension Credit or certain other income-related benefits and, in England and Wales, to households in which a pensioner’s taxable income is below a fixed threshold. It changes for pensioner (b) in 2036-37 on the middle and 90th-percentile paths, where the plan’s lower pension brings that pensioner’s taxable income under the threshold and the payment of £200 offsets part of the difference.

On the 90th-percentile path the council renter shows a larger step. In 2039-40 its State Pension is £1,552 lower under the plan, which brings it onto the Pension Credit guarantee credit. A pensioner receiving the guarantee credit has no income counted in the Housing Benefit means test, so its Housing Benefit rises by £1,504 to the maximum. Its income rises by £1,390. The step is this large partly because of how the runs uprate. They raise the Pension Credit guarantee with earnings, as the law requires at least, while policyengine-uk raises the Housing Benefit personal allowance for pensioners with CPI. The allowance starts above the guarantee. On paths where earnings outgrow prices the guarantee overtakes it. From then on a pensioner moving onto guarantee credit moves from a tapered Housing Benefit award to the maximum in one step. This example claims Pension Credit, so it receives the guarantee credit and the step is real in law. In the survey runs the step now happens only for a household that receives the guarantee credit, not one that is merely entitled to it (Section 8).

8 Method and limitations

8.1 A path through PolicyEngine UK

A path is calendar-year CPI and earnings growth for 2027–2039, plus the statutory inputs that set the April rises. The calendar growth replaces the model’s OBR growth assumptions for CPI and average earnings, with RPI and CPIH moved by the same amount as CPI. The statutory inputs, September CPI and May–July earnings growth, replace the model’s own, from which policyengine-uk builds its triple lock. Both enter in a scenario applied before the data load. The model builds its derived series (uprating indices, the lagged CPI that uprates benefits, its own triple lock) when it loads parameters, so the same changes passed as a reform after loading would do nothing. policyengine-uk’s derived series now run to 2039-40 and beyond by themselves. The analysis extends only the uprating of private pensions, which policyengine-uk still stops at 2034.

Each run checks, in every year, that the model follows the path, and fails if any check misses. Benefit rates uprated by CPI must follow the path’s calendar-year CPI of the year before. In law September CPI uprates them, so this is a simplification. CPI-indexed thresholds must follow the path in the same year, and employment income must follow the path’s earnings. The model’s own statutory inputs must be the path’s, and its own triple lock must be the larger of them and the floor. Every flat-rate pension must scale exactly by the ratio of the two rules’ amounts. The model’s adjustment of pay for changes in employer National Insurance must be zero. The Pension Credit guarantee must follow the path’s earnings. The variables the net saving is split into must add up to the model’s own government balance. A separate identity must hold too: the government’s balance plus households’ net income must equal market income less pension contributions, after adjusting for items counted on only one side. Private pension income moves with the previous year’s RPI up to a cap. Dividend, property, savings and self-employment income, rents and council tax do not follow the path, and each run records their growth. April 2027’s benefit uprating is the model’s own forecast of calendar-2026 CPI on every path, not September 2026 CPI.

8.2 The model and data release

Every run uses one release bundle: policyengine 6.2.6, which pins policyengine-uk 2.125.1 and policyengine-core 3.33.0, with the Enhanced FRS for 2024-25, release 1.58.0. policyengine.py publishes the bundle and states that this data release works with this model release. The data release carries the Pension Credit and pension-age Housing Benefit fixes made to the data after the earlier pilot. Each run records the installed versions and the dataset’s hash. The build refuses to start any model job unless the installed packages, the dataset’s bytes and the release manifest all match the bundle. An earlier pilot of this method ran on policyengine-uk 2.120.0 with an older data release. That pair was never released together, so we quote none of its figures.

One change in the bundle matters directly for the net saving. Housing Benefit treats a person who receives the Pension Credit guarantee credit as having no income or capital to count, a passport to the maximum award. The law keys this on receipt of the guarantee credit. Until policyengine-uk 2.123.6 the model keyed it on entitlement, so a pensioner entitled to the guarantee credit but not claiming it still got the passport (policyengine-uk #1927). In our runs a lower pension makes some households newly entitled to the guarantee credit. Keyed on entitlement, they moved straight to the maximum Housing Benefit, which overstated the Housing Benefit offset. The bundle keys all three parts of the passport (applicable income, tariff income and capital) on receipt. Before any model job the build tests this on synthetic pensioners. One is entitled to the guarantee credit but does not claim it and must face the ordinary Housing Benefit means test. One claims it and must get the maximum award. The check also reverses the receipt flag, to confirm that every part of the passport reads it. If any check fails, the build stops.

8.3 The population in each year

The survey is a sample of households in 2024-25. We age it statically. Each record stands for a person of its age in every year, not a survivor aged forward. So a record aged seventy-five stands for someone aged seventy-five in 2024-25 and for someone aged seventy-five in 2039-40. Three things change from year to year: the weight each household carries, the age a top-coded record represents, and each person’s State Pension type.

Enhanced FRS ages are top-coded at 80. We give each top-coded record a represented age from 80 to 105. Within each sex the records are put in a fixed order by a hash of the data, and their weights are split across ages in proportion to the ONS’s single-year population shares. The weighted number of people aged 80 and over does not change. Every record also gets a fixed birthday within its year of age, drawn as policyengine-uk draws it, so records that keep their survey age keep the model’s own birthday and pension type. Anything the model derived from the survey ages is cleared, so the model’s own State Pension age and every reader of it see the represented ages.

Household weights are the dataset’s own up to 2025, the year the data release calibrates its weights to. After that we reweight households so that each sex and age group grows as it does in the ONS’s 2024-based principal population projection for the UK (Office for National Statistics 2026a). The groups are five-year bands up to 59, single years from 60 to 79, then 80 to 84, 85 to 89, and 90 and over. A fiscal year’s population is three-quarters of the mid-year population of its first year and a quarter of the next. The targets are the survey’s own counts in 2025 grown at the ONS rates, not the ONS levels. The reweighting finds the weights closest to the 2025 ones, in relative entropy, that hit every target. No weight may fall below a fifth of its 2025 value or rise above five times it. If a target cannot be reached within those bounds, the run fails. Person and benefit-unit weights follow their household’s.

Each person’s State Pension type follows from their date of birth, from the represented age and birthday in each year. Men born before 6 April 1951 and women born before 6 April 1953 reached State Pension age before 6 April 2016 and are on the basic State Pension. People born on or after those dates are on the new State Pension, and people below State Pension age have none. So in later years more pensioners are on the new State Pension. A test checks that these types equal policyengine-uk’s own on the same ages and birthdays. State Pension age is the model’s own, set from each person’s date of birth by the law’s timetable, including the rise to 67 for people born on or after 6 April 1960 (“Pensions Act” 2014). Because ages are held, a record’s date of birth moves a year later each year. People aged 66 are partly over State Pension age in 2026-27 and 2027-28 and below it from 2028-29.

The State Pension a record reports in the survey has to be split between these types each year. The reported amount counts only for people over State Pension age in the survey year, since nobody is paid a State Pension before it. A few records below that age report one. They are reporting errors, and the model already pays them nothing. In each year the counted amount is split by that year’s type at the type’s survey-year flat rate. The flat-rate part moves with each rule. The rest is the additional State Pension (and, on the new State Pension, protected payments). We hold it at its survey-year amount, grown by September CPI. Splitting by each year’s type matters. Split by the survey year’s type, a record moved from the basic to the new State Pension would be paid the difference between the two flat rates twice, once inside its new State Pension and again as additional pension. Every run checks that, in the survey year, basic, new and additional State Pension add up to the counted report for every person, to £0.01.

A record that reported a basic State Pension and is a new State Pension recipient in a later year raises a question: what amount should it get? By default it keeps its reported amount, split at the new type’s flat rate. As a bound, a sensitivity raises only that record’s flat-rate part to the full new State Pension under each rule. We cannot do better from the survey. In law the new State Pension of someone with pre-2016 contributions starts from a foundation amount: the higher of the old-system and new-system calculations, less any contracting-out deduction, plus later qualifying years up to the full rate (“Pensions Act” 2014). That needs each person’s qualifying years before and after 2016, their additional pension history and their contracting-out record. The Enhanced FRS has none of these. It has only the total State Pension each person reports. So the law does not fix an amount for a re-typed record here. The full-rate bound limits the flat-rate part only. It is not an estimate of the transitional entitlement, and not a confidence bound. Every result in this release keeps the reported amount; the full-rate bound has not yet been rerun on this release bundle and will be reported with the post-Budget release.

We measure what each piece of the ageing does with paired full runs on the same paths. One treatment holds the survey weights and types with represented ages. One adds the ONS reweighting, one adds the cohort types, and one adds both, which is what every result in this paper uses. The difference between the last and the sum of the middle two is their interaction. A further control reweights only to the ONS total population, not by age and sex. The four-way design has not yet been rerun on this release bundle; its results will follow with the post-Budget release.

8.4 Inputs held the same under both rules

Four inputs are set the same way under both rules, so that only the basic and new State Pension differ between the two runs of a path. The first is each person’s State Pension type, from their date of birth as above. Each run reads the types back from the model every year and fails if any differs. The second is the additional State Pension, at its survey-year amount grown by September CPI (published to April 2026, then the path’s), for people over State Pension age that year. policyengine-uk still scales the additional pension by the ratio of the flat rates (policyengine-uk #1941), so lower flat rates would lower it too. We hold it fixed instead. The third is State Pension age, the model’s own as above. The fourth is the Pension Credit guarantee. It rises with May–July earnings and is never cut, the minimum the law requires (“Social Security Administration Act” 1992). policyengine-uk uprates it by CPI. This is an assumption, the same under both rules.

8.5 Jobs and reproducibility

Each model run is a job in its own process. It is cached under a hash of its arguments, the analysis code and the package versions, so an interrupted build resumes and any change to the engine reruns every job. The results in this paper were built at revision 8838fd4 of PolicyEngine/uk-triple-lock, with the release bundle above. The manuscript reads data/results.json and computes every number it prints from it. The repository’s README gives the commands to rebuild the file. The results file gives no survey record’s identifier, weight or amount, and no single record’s contribution to a total, because FRS records are licensed data.

8.6 Limitations

Our results depend first on how the plan is defined. The speech gave no formula. We read DWP’s description as a rule that, from April 2030, raises the pension by at least the higher of CPI and 2.5%, and by whatever more keeps it at its 2029-30 ratio to earnings, with the top-up rounded up to 0.1 point. Every rule follows the triple lock to April 2029, and each rise uses the triple lock’s measures, September CPI and May–July earnings, although the statute requires only the earnings link. Only the basic and new State Pension differ between the rules. Under both, the additional State Pension rises with September CPI, the Pension Credit guarantee with May–July earnings (its statutory minimum), and State Pension age follows the law’s timetable by date of birth. Costs are in cash terms, for the UK and for Great Britain, and we model no behavioural response.

Second, any statement about uncertainty rests on one statistical model of prices and earnings, a monthly VAR fitted to 2000–2026 and shifted so that its calendar-year averages equal the OBR’s. No form of the model passed the screen we first set, because of the 2021 furlough base effect. We changed the screen after seeing those scores, to score April 2022 as the law applied it. We registered the new rule before scoring it, and we report the first failure beside it, but the change was still made after the fact. The model passes the new screen in a dry run on today’s data; the binding score uses the 28 October 2026 Budget forecast. The expected saving is conditional on that model and is not a forecast probability, and the single scenarios are named cases, not a range for the saving. The backtests rest on six and twelve overlapping four-year windows, using today’s revised data rather than the figures available at the time. Its statutory inputs depart from the OBR’s quarterly figures before the switch. After 2030 the central path follows the OBR’s long-term path, which the OBR describes as not a forecast. In every run April 2027’s benefit uprating uses the model’s calendar-2026 CPI forecast, not the September 2026 figure.

Third, the population is aged statically, not projected. Each record stands for a person of its age in every year. Ages above the survey’s top code of 80 are assigned, not observed. The weights follow the ONS projection by age and sex, and pension types follow date of birth, but later pensioners’ amounts come from today’s records of the same age. Deaths, migration and contribution histories are not simulated, and pensioners living abroad are outside the survey. A record that moves from the basic to the new State Pension keeps its reported amount by default. Its legal amount cannot be rebuilt from the survey, so the full new State Pension is shown only as a bound on the flat-rate part. The data also carry a known weight gap (policyengine-uk-data #538): the data build calibrates its weights to 2025, but policyengine-uk uprates them back to 2025 by its own population growth, so every 2025 total is 0.44% low while the relative weights are exact. Our ageing keeps the model’s own 2025 weights and grows them from there, so it carries the gap into every later year, neither adding to it nor closing it. The fix belongs to the data build or the model. Rents and council tax are held at their 2030 amounts after 2030, and dividend, property, savings and self-employment income do not follow the simulated path.

Fourth, we can check the data against DWP only in part. DWP’s tables match our Great Britain figures only to 2030-31, where they stop. They give no matched figure for the basic and new State Pension separately, and no forecast by age or by country. Recipient counts, the split between basic and new State Pension, and forecasts are not published by country, so a country check cannot be done from published data.

Finally, the net figures, though not the gross ones, depend on how policyengine-uk models the benefits that respond to a lower pension. In the survey runs Housing Benefit and council tax reduction respond only for households already receiving them, so nobody the plan makes newly entitled starts claiming. This understates the offsets and overstates the net saving. The Housing Benefit passport for guarantee credit recipients now keys on receipt, as in law. But the runs raise the Pension Credit guarantee with earnings while policyengine-uk raises the pensioner Housing Benefit personal allowance with CPI. On paths where earnings outgrow prices the guarantee overtakes the allowance. A pensioner who then starts receiving the guarantee credit gains the maximum Housing Benefit in one step, so a small pension cut can move one heavily weighted survey household’s income by a large amount. policyengine-uk does not model the basic State Pension’s age addition at 80 (policyengine-uk #2139), and it pays Scotland’s Pension Age Winter Heating Payment at a flat household rate rather than the rates in the regulations (policyengine-uk #2138).

9 Appendix: the tests on past forecasts

This appendix gives the scores behind the screen of Section 2.5, then an earlier, wider backtest kept as the record.

Table 16 gives the dry-run scores of the screen on today’s inputs, for each of the five forms in tests A and B. April 2022 is scored as the law applied it (earnings set equal to CPI), which decides the verdict, and as published, which is reported beside it and has no say. Coverage is the share of outcomes inside the form’s central 80% band: of the yearly gaps between earnings and CPI (“annual”) and of the gap the two rules open after four rises (“final years”). The biases are the form’s mean minus what happened: in the yearly gap (percentage points), in the number of changes of lead per path, and in the share of years with earnings below the floor. A form must pass every condition in both tests; the verdict column gives the screen’s result for the form as a whole.

Before that screen we ran a wider backtest, kept here as the earlier record. It is why the paper reports an expected value and scenarios, not the probability of any outcome. We scored nine ways of putting a range on the two statutory inputs, and the OBR forecast alone, against what followed six OBR forecasts (the March 2016 EFO to the March 2021 EFO): the forecasts with at least two earlier forecasts whose target years had all been published, which the method built on past forecast errors needs. Each method is fitted only on data dated before the forecast, using today’s revised figures, and scored on the September CPI and May–July earnings for the four years after the forecast year, which set the April rises two to five years after it.

The scores are proper scoring rules (Gneiting and Raftery 2007): the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) for the gap between the triple lock and the Burnham plan after those rises, in points of the pension; the energy score for the two series over the years together; and the variogram score (Scheuerer and Hamill 2015), which rewards getting the dependence between years right. Lower is better for each. We also record whether the realised gap fell inside each method’s middle 80%, and how many changes of lead between CPI and earnings the method expected against how many happened.

Table 17 gives each method’s score for the gap and its coverage, with April 2022 scored both ways; how April 2022 is scored decides the result. Scored on the published May–July 2021 earnings growth of 8.4%, every method expected fewer changes of lead than happened, and the best placed one of six realised gaps in its middle 80%. Scored with earnings equal to CPI (3.1%) that April, as the law set it when the earnings leg was suspended, the best placed four of six; the best-scoring of those (monthly model, not calibrated) missed the March 2020 EFO and March 2021 EFO forecasts, whose rises include April 2023, set by September CPI of 10.1%. The method built on past OBR forecast errors has only 2–7 paths at these forecasts, so its middle 80% is the full range of those paths.
