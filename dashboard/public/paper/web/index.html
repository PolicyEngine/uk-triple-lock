Abstract

We study the UK’s adjusted Triple Lock: from April 2030 the State Pension rises by the highest of 2.5%, CPI and the amount needed to keep its April 2029 value relative to earnings. Its excess over its earnings path follows a reflected (Lindley) recursion, so its gap from the triple lock grows only in years in which earnings lead after prices or the floor did. For the UK in 2039-40 it lowers State Pension spending by £0.5bn on the Office for Budget Responsibility’s (OBR) central path; by £10.9bn under the OBR’s long-term triple-lock convention, which gives the adjusted rule no premium of its own; and by £11.4bn or £11.9bn, estimated from 160 full runs, on average over simulated paths with earnings growth 0.5 points lower or higher, from a model that failed a test on past forecasts, whose rule we registered before scoring under it, by overstating how often earnings growth falls below 2.5%. These are scenarios, not an expected value, beside DWP’s £15bn for Great Britain; reweighting the paths to the realised frequency of such years lowers their gap. On the OBR long-term path, losing households have £801 a year less, and pensioner poverty after housing costs rises by 0.70 to 0.75 points, and by 1.52 on the simulated path near the 90th percentile of the gap.

Keywords: pension indexation; triple lock; path dependence; microsimulation; forecast evaluation; proper scoring rules; registered analysis plans. JEL codes: H55, H68, C53, D31.

1 Introduction

Indexation rules set how pensions in payment keep pace with prices and earnings, and with them much of a pension system’s long-run cost (Vidal-Meliá et al. 2009; OECD 2023). A rule that pays the highest of several indices each year is asymmetric: it passes on every year in which one index leads and keeps that gain in all later years. This paper asks which part of such a rule’s excess over earnings an earnings-anchored rule removes, on which paths of the indices, and what can be reported when the model of the indices’ variation fails a test against past forecasts.

Since April 2011 the triple lock has raised the State Pension each April by the highest of prices, earnings growth and 2.5% (prices measured by RPI in its first year and by CPI since), except in April 2022, when the law suspended the earnings leg (Section 2). Because it takes the highest of three figures every year, a year in which prices or the floor run ahead of earnings raises the pension for good, and later rises build on the higher level. The OBR’s long-term projections assume that the triple lock raises the pension 0.6 percentage points a year faster than earnings (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026c), and it estimates that by 2029-30 the triple lock will have added £15.5 billion a year to spending relative to earnings uprating since its introduction (Office for Budget Responsibility 2025).

The Budget of 28 October 2026 announced the adjusted Triple Lock, to take effect from the next Parliament: the triple lock applies to each rise through April 2029, and from April 2030 the pension also falls back towards its April 2029 value relative to earnings after years in which prices or the floor beat earnings (Section 3.1). The Budget puts the saving at: “Adjusting the Triple Lock is estimated to reduce State Pension spending by £15 billion a year by the late 2030s, and by £50 billion a year by the late 2040s” (HM Treasury 2026, para. 1.29), citing DWP’s analysis of the change (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b), and it recognises that the saving depends on the path of the indices: “Under the Triple Lock, the cost of the State Pension depends on which of prices, earnings or 2.5% is highest in each year, and on how those differences compound over time. That makes it inherently hard to forecast” (HM Treasury 2026, Box 5.A). We quantify that dependence.

The adjusted Triple Lock keeps the triple lock’s protection against prices and the floor but removes the ratchet: the part of the triple lock’s excess over earnings that it keeps after earnings lead again. The adjusted rule still pays above its earnings path while an excess lasts, so its saving is less than the triple lock’s whole premium over earnings uprating. We estimate its effect against the triple lock from 2027-28 to 2039-40 with full runs of PolicyEngine UK (Ahmadi et al. 2026) on the whole survey population, on the OBR’s central path, under the OBR’s long-term triple-lock convention and on simulated paths of prices and earnings.

The main result is that the share of the triple lock’s excess over earnings that the adjusted rule removes depends on the order of the indices’ leads: all of it on the OBR’s central and long-term paths, 42% of it replayed from April 2012 on past inputs, and 51% and 78% on average on the two simulated scenarios, where most of the saving comes from year-to-year variation rather than from the paths’ mean.

The paper makes three contributions. First, we show that the earnings-anchored rule’s excess over its earnings path is a cumulative sum reflected at zero, a Lindley recursion (Lindley 1952), so its gap from the max-of-indices rule depends on the running minimum of the cumulative difference between the indices, as a lookback option’s payoff does (Goldman et al. 1979). The identity is standard; applied to indexation it gives the gap’s yearly increment, an upper bound by the smaller of the two indices’ cumulative leads, and the exact condition for a positive saving: a year in which prices or the floor lead, followed later by a year in which earnings lead (Section 3.3). Second, we measure how much of the gross saving the means-tested offsets and income tax absorb, and where the remainder falls: on the OBR long-term path the government keeps 68% of the gross saving (Section 5.4), and the loss relative to income is largest in the lower-middle deciles (Section 5.5). Third, we test the model of prices and earnings on past OBR forecasts with proper scoring rules (Gneiting and Raftery 2007; Scheuerer and Hamill 2015), under a pass rule registered before the first scores and a reporting rule registered before the second (Section 4.4) (Casey et al. 2012; Olken 2015), and show what a failed condition leaves: a scenario envelope rather than an expected value.

The saving is small on the OBR central path, on which earnings lead in almost every year, and much larger on the simulated scenarios, which differ from the central path in both the mean and the year-to-year variation of earnings growth; on a single path through each scenario’s mean inputs the gap is a small fraction of the scenario’s average (Section 5.1). Under forecast uncertainty we can bound the cost under the OBR’s convention and report scenarios, but our test does not license an expected value.

Section 2 describes the triple lock’s record and relates the paper to the literature. Section 3 states the two rules, derives where the saving comes from and replays the rule on past inputs. Section 4 describes the data, the microsimulation, the paths of prices and earnings and the registered test. Section 5 gives the fiscal effects, the mechanism along single paths and the distributional effects. Section 6 compares the results with official costings, Section 7 discusses what they imply for costing such rules, and Section 8 concludes and states the main limitations.

2 Background and related literature

2.1 The triple lock since 2011

Figure 1 shows the rises actually paid in each April from 2011 to 2026. April 2011’s rise, 4.6%, was set by RPI under that year’s up-rating order (Social Security Benefits Up-rating Order 2011), while the replay of the CPI rule gives 3.1%. From 2012, CPI set the rise five times, earnings six times and the floor four times. In April 2022 the law suspended the earnings leg, whose input was 8.4% on today’s figures, so that year’s rise was CPI, 3.1% (Social Security (Up-rating of Benefits) Act 2021). Over the 16 rises the pension rose by 89.4% as paid.

2.2 Related literature

Automatic adjustment mechanisms link a pay-as-you-go pension system’s benefits or contributions to indicators, so that its finances respond to demographic and economic change without new legislation (Vidal-Meliá et al. 2009); Knell (2010) shows how the choice of adjustment factor changes the return such a system pays. The indexation of pensions in payment is one such link, and the OECD’s review of the inflation surge since 2021 records how far different rules protected pensioners’ purchasing power (OECD 2023). The triple lock is unusual in taking the highest of three figures each year, and Portes (2023) places it in the longer history of the State Pension’s value relative to earnings. A rule that pays the higher of two growth rates and compounds the result has the structure of a path-dependent option. The triple lock’s excess over earnings, the sum of each year’s lead of prices or the floor over earnings, compounds a yearly local floor, as a cliquet or ratchet guarantee does (Hardy 2003; Tiong 2000), while the adjusted rule’s gap depends on a running minimum of the cumulative difference between the indices, as a lookback option’s payoff depends on the running minimum of a price (Goldman et al. 1979; Conze and Viswanathan 1991).

The option analogy connects the paper to the valuation of guarantees. Merton (1977) values a loan guarantee as a put option, Lucas (2012) argues that public guarantees and policies should be valued at market prices that charge for the risk they carry, and Geanakoplos and Zeldes (2010) apply that approach to accrued Social Security benefits. In pension finance, Jong (2008) values the liabilities of a fund that indexes benefits only when its funding allows, an option embedded in the indexation rule. We measure the cost in cash, undiscounted and with no charge for risk, because that is the measure in which official costings of uprating rules are stated and the paper compares with them; a market value of the triple lock’s guarantee would also need a price for UK earnings and inflation risk, which we leave aside. We use the option analogy for the structure of the payoff only.

The same guarantee can be read as risk sharing between generations. Indexation decides who bears the risk in a pay-as-you-go system’s returns: Shiller (1999) argues for designing social security to share income risk across generations, Bohn (2009) shows how fiscal policy and benefit rules allocate aggregate risk between cohorts, and Auerbach and Lee (2011) compare how sustainable unfunded systems with different adjustment rules spread demographic and economic shocks. The triple lock insures pensioners against years in which prices or the floor beat earnings, permanently and at the expense of later taxpayers; the adjusted rule keeps that insurance only until earnings catch up, and so moves the risk of a fall in the pension relative to earnings back to pensioners for a time. We measure the fiscal side of that transfer, not its welfare value.

Long-run projections of pension spending are uncertain, and stochastic projections of social security finances express that uncertainty as distributions over paths of demographic and economic inputs (Lee and Tuljapurkar 1998). The OBR’s fiscal risks and sustainability reports treat the triple lock as a source of fiscal risk and project its cost against earnings uprating (Office for Budget Responsibility 2025); in the long-term scenarios of its 2026 Fiscal risks and sustainability report, as summarised in the October 2026 outlook, the triple lock accounted for around a third of the projected rise in State Pension spending as a share of GDP (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a, para. 3.48). Central banks and fiscal analysts publish fan charts to show the uncertainty around a central projection (Britton et al. 1998; Celasun et al. 2006). A model that supplies such a distribution can be tested against outcomes: probability integral transforms check its calibration (Diebold et al. 1998), a forecast should be as sharp as its calibration allows (Gneiting et al. 2007; Mitchell and Wallis 2011), and proper scoring rules reward a forecaster for reporting its true predictive distribution (Gneiting and Raftery 2007), including for multivariate outcomes (Scheuerer and Hamill 2015). Density forecasts of inflation and output are better calibrated when they allow volatility to change over time (Clark 2011), which our model does not. Official fiscal forecasts have their own evaluation literature, which scores their errors and biases after the event (Leal et al. 2008); we know of no earlier test of a stochastic model of uprating inputs against official forecast vintages before its paths are used for costing.

Static tax-benefit microsimulation applies the rules of the tax and benefit system to a representative survey of households, with behaviour held fixed, and is the standard tool for the distributional analysis of benefit reforms (Sutherland and Figari 2013); UKMOD is one such model for the four nations of the UK (Richiardi et al. 2021). Dynamic microsimulation models instead project individual life courses, including pension entitlements, forward in time (Li and O’Donoghue 2013); DWP’s costing of the adjusted Triple Lock uses one (Section 6). The net saving depends on how many newly entitled pensioners claim Pension Credit, and take-up of means-tested benefits responds to the size of the entitlement and the cost of claiming (Pudney et al. 2006); our runs hold each benefit unit’s claim decision fixed. Pre-analysis plans fix a study’s hypotheses and tests before the data are seen, so that results cannot be chosen after the fact (Casey et al. 2012; Olken 2015), and Christensen and Miguel (2018) review their spread in economics. They are rare in model-based policy costing, where the analyst chooses both the model and its evaluation; Section 4.4 describes the plan we registered and how it changed after the first scores.

3 Policy rules

3.1 The two rules

Under the triple lock the full basic and new State Pension rise each April by the highest of three figures: the CPI 12-month rate for the previous September (Office for National Statistics 2026c); growth in average weekly earnings, whole-economy total pay, in May to July of the previous year (Office for National Statistics 2026b); and 2.5%. ONS publishes both inputs to 0.1 point, and both rules here use them at that precision. We index each input by the April it sets: ctc_t is the September CPI rate and ete_t the May–July earnings growth published before April tt, and ff is the 2.5% floor. Fiscal year tt pays the level set in April tt.

The adjusted Triple Lock follows the triple lock to April 2029. From April 2030 let LtL_t be the pension’s level and AtA_t an earnings path that starts at the 2029-30 level and grows with May–July earnings, At=At−1(1+et)A_t = A_{t-1}(1 + e_t). Then

Lt=max⁡(Lt−1(1+max⁡(ct,f)),At).L_t = \max\bigl(L_{t-1}\,(1 + \max(c_t, f)),\; A_t\bigr).

The pension never rises by less than the higher of CPI and the floor, and never falls below its earnings path. In April 2030 this rise equals the triple lock’s, since the earnings path starts at the same level, so the two rules can first differ in April 2031 and the saving in 2030-31 is zero on every path. As a modelling assumption we round any top-up to the earnings path up to the next 0.1 points, so the pension never ends below that path; a top-up therefore leaves the pension at most 0.1% above its earnings path, and the margin does not accumulate, because each top-up is set afresh against AtA_t.

The Budget states the rule: from April 2030 the State Pension rises “by the highest of 2.5%, inflation, or the amount required to maintain its value relative to earnings as reached in April 2029” (HM Treasury 2026, para. 5.23 and Box 5.A), and adds that “It removes the ‘ratchet’ effect, whereby the State Pension rises ever higher relative to earnings” (HM Treasury 2026, ch. 1). Its wording fixes the anchor: the pension’s value relative to earnings “as reached in April 2029”. DWP’s description says the same in other words: the pension will “reach a record high relative to earnings over the coming three years”, and from April 2030 it keeps pensioners benefiting “via the State Pension retaining that record high value relative to earnings”: “In any given year it will go up by at least inflation or 2.5% – and anything more that is needed to retain that value”. It adds: “This means that over time the State Pension is set to rise in line with average earnings” (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b). Two choices remain ours. The rule uses the triple lock’s measures, September CPI and May–July earnings, and the top-up is rounded up, as above.

The government announced on 29 September 2026 that it would keep the triple lock for each rise through April 2029 and adjust it from April 2030, and DWP published its analysis the same day (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b). The OBR writes that its estimates of the change “depend critically on the order, level and covariation of wage growth and inflation over time” (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a, para. 3.53). The law requires only that the basic and new State Pension rise at least in line with earnings, in Great Britain under section 150A of the Social Security Administration Act 1992 (Social Security Administration Act 1992), and in Northern Ireland by a corresponding order under section 132A of the Social Security Administration (Northern Ireland) Act 1992 (Social Security Administration (Northern Ireland) Act 1992); the prices leg and the floor are policy on top. In a year when the adjusted Triple Lock rises by the higher of CPI and the floor while its level returns towards its earnings path, its rise is below earnings growth, which section 150A does not allow, so the rule needs primary legislation amending section 150A and its Northern Ireland counterpart, as the suspension of the earnings leg for April 2022 did (Social Security (Up-rating of Benefits) Act 2021). On both OBR scenarios this happens once, in April 2032; on the model paths near the median and the 90th percentile of the gap, in four and five of the Aprils from 2031 to 2039.

3.2 Where the saving comes from

When earnings lead, both rules give the same rise. The rules part only after a year in which the higher of CPI and 2.5% beats earnings. Both pay that higher figure, but it lifts the adjusted Triple Lock above its earnings path. When earnings lead again, the triple lock rises with earnings from the higher level, while the adjusted Triple Lock rises only by the higher of CPI and the floor until its earnings path catches up. Under the triple lock each such rise stays in the pension’s level; under the adjusted rule the level returns to its earnings path over the following years.

A two-year example shows the arithmetic. Suppose CPI is 6% and earnings growth 2% in the first year, then CPI 2% and earnings 4%. The triple lock rises by 6% and then 4%, 10.24% in all. The adjusted Triple Lock also rises by 6% in the first year, which leaves it 3.85 log points above its earnings path, and then by the floor, 2.5%, 8.65% in all. In the second year earnings lead by 1.45 log points, less than the excess, so the whole lead becomes the gap: the adjusted rule ends 1.4% below the triple lock (1.59 points less cumulative growth). The next subsection states this in general.

3.4 The rule on past inputs

The decomposition can be checked on the inputs that have already occurred, with no model of their future path. The replay uses today’s revised ONS figures, so that both rules see the same inputs. It differs from the rise paid in April 2016, 2019, 2020 and 2025, by 0.1 to 0.3 points, because earnings figures have since been revised, and in April 2011, which RPI set. In April 2022, when the law suspended the earnings leg, the replay’s earnings path grows by that year’s CPI, 3.1%, the figure the rise followed, rather than by the published earnings figure. On the replay the triple lock rose 87.4%, against 66.2% for earnings alone and 60.2% for prices alone, which leaves it 12.7% above where an earnings link alone would have taken it.

4 Data and method

4.1 Model and data

Every saving and household figure for a given path is a full run of PolicyEngine UK (Ahmadi et al. 2026; PolicyEngine 2026b, 2026c) on the whole survey population, once under each rule. PolicyEngine UK is a static tax-benefit microsimulation model of the kind Sutherland and Figari (2013) describe: it applies the tax and benefit rules of each year to survey households and allows no behavioural response. All runs use policyengine 6.11.1, which pins policyengine-uk 2.151.0 and policyengine-core 3.33.3, with the national Microcosm dataset for 2024-25 published with it (78,234 households) (PolicyEngine 2026a). The Enhanced Family Resources Survey, version 1.58.2, runs only for the check against DWP’s caseloads (Table 8). A path’s CPI and earnings growth replace the model’s economic assumptions before the data load, so benefit rates, tax thresholds and earnings follow it, and every run checks that they do. Only the basic and new State Pension differ between the two runs of a path. Under both rules the additional State Pension rises with September CPI and State Pension age follows the law’s timetable. Pension-age Housing Benefit is modelled as under current law. Money is in cash terms for the UK unless stated.

Three conventions shape the net saving. First, the Pension Credit guarantee rises with May–July earnings, the minimum section 150A requires, under both rules. In each April since 2022 the guarantee has in practice risen by the same percentage as the State Pension, as in April 2023, when both rose by 10.1% against earnings growth of 5.5% (Social Security Benefits Up-rating Order 2023); in earlier years in which prices or the floor set the State Pension’s rise it sometimes rose by less, by the basic State Pension’s cash increase in April 2013 and 2021 and by about earnings in April 2018 (Social Security Benefits Up-rating Order 2013, 2018, 2021). Neither the Budget nor DWP’s analysis says how the guarantee will be uprated under the adjusted Triple Lock. If it rose by the same percentage as the State Pension under each rule, it would be lower under the adjusted rule, the Pension Credit offset would be smaller and the net saving larger (Section 5.4 bounds the effect). Second, the income tax personal allowance stays at £12,570 to 2030-31, as current law sets it, and then rises with each path’s September CPI; from April 2031, when the rules can first differ, the full new State Pension is above it, so a lower pension means less income tax even for a pensioner with no other income. The Budget’s Pension Tax Allowance removes income tax for pensioners whose total income is at or below the full new State Pension from April 2027 to April 2030 (HM Treasury 2026, Table 6.1); it lapses before the two rules can first differ, so it does not affect the differences reported here. The Budget leaves arrangements beyond April 2030 open; we assume no successor. A successor tied to the full new State Pension would scale with each rule’s pension and reduce the income tax offset for pensioners with incomes near that level. Third, Pension Credit take-up follows the dataset’s own claim decision for each benefit unit, so a household newly entitled under the adjusted rule claims only if that decision says it would; Housing Benefit and council tax reduction respond only for households already receiving them, and passporting from the guarantee credit follows actual receipt.

4.2 The population in each year

The dataset represents households in 2024-25, and each record stands for a person of its age in every year. Through 2025, the year the dataset calibrates to, each record keeps its own weight; after it, household weights are reweighted so that each age and sex group grows as in the ONS’s 2024-based population projection (Office for National Statistics 2026e). Ages are not top-coded, so every recorded age is kept. Each person’s State Pension type follows from the date of birth that their age implies in each year: those who reach State Pension age before 6 April 2016 are on the basic State Pension, later cohorts on the new one (Pensions Act 2014). A record of a given age therefore moves from the basic to the new State Pension as later cohorts reach that age. Deaths, migration and contribution histories are not simulated, and pensioners abroad are outside the dataset. Records carried forward from today’s pensioners may understate the entitlements of people reaching State Pension age in the 2030s, most of whom will have spent their whole working lives under the new State Pension.

4.3 Paths of prices and earnings

The OBR central path uses the OBR’s October 2026 Economic and fiscal outlook, published with the Budget, for calendar years to 2030 (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a), and the OBR’s March 2026 long-term economic determinants after that (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026c), which the OBR describes as a projection, not a forecast; the OBR published no long-term determinants with its October forecast. We call the combination the OBR central path. April 2027’s State Pension rise uses the published May–July 2026 earnings growth, 3.9%, and September 2026 CPI, 3.5% (Office for National Statistics 2026b, 2026c). For the Aprils 2028 to 2031 the inputs are the OBR’s September-quarter CPI and April–June earnings growth; later Aprils use its calendar-year figures. The OBR forecasts average earnings on a national-accounts measure, which stands in here for May–July average weekly earnings. Both rules share these inputs.

The simulated paths come from a model of monthly changes in the CPI index (Office for National Statistics 2026d) and in average weekly earnings (Office for National Statistics 2026a), fitted to data from 2000 to the latest published month, with the furlough months of 2020–21 left out. Each month’s change depends on the previous month’s changes in both series, with fixed coefficients; the shocks are past pairs of residuals drawn at random, so prices and earnings keep their joint variation, but the model has no periods of higher or lower volatility and no uncertainty about its coefficients. From the simulated months come both the statutory inputs (September CPI, May–July earnings growth) and the calendar-year growth the rest of the model uses. Every path is then shifted by the same smooth drift so that the average calendar-year growth matches the OBR’s forecast in every year, while each path keeps its own ups and downs. The earnings scenarios move that drift by 0.5 points a year from 2031.

The shift matches calendar-year means only, so the statutory inputs that drive both rules are centred off the central path’s (Table 2). Over the Aprils 2031 to 2039 the scenarios’ mean September CPI is 0.11 points below the central path’s, and their mean May–July earnings growth is 0.50 points below it with lower earnings growth and 0.38 points above it with higher, so the two scenarios are not centred on the central path. The simulated paths also have earnings growth below 2.5% in 43.0% and 26.1% of those Aprils, beside the 44.6% that the model expected and the 17.9% realised in the test on past forecasts (Section 4.4). 4.4 Testing the model on past forecasts

An average over the simulated paths needs a model whose paths behave like the real inputs, so we registered a test on past OBR forecasts (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026b) before scoring any model. Five versions of the model of prices and earnings are fitted only on data available before each forecast, centred on that forecast, and scored, with proper scoring rules for each statistic and for the joint outcome (Gneiting and Raftery 2007; Scheuerer and Hamill 2015) and with the coverage of central bands, on the gap between earnings and CPI in each year, on how often the lead changes, on how often earnings growth falls below 2.5% and on the gap the two rules open after four rises. One test uses seven forecasts, one a year from the March 2016 EFO to the March 2022 EFO; another uses thirteen, from the June 2010 Budget forecast to the March 2022 EFO. Table 3 gives the pass rule; the thresholds were fixed together, as a broad screen, before any score was seen, and the record of the rule gives no other basis for any one of them. The accuracy condition compares versions with each other, so the main version meets it by construction, and its calibration rests on the coverage and bias conditions.

The rule changed once, after the first scores and before the second. Under the first rule every version failed, because April 2022 was scored on the published earnings: each had only one of six four-year outcomes inside its band, because the furlough schemes distorted 2021 pay growth. The second rule differs from the first in two elements, both fixed after we had seen the first scores: it scores April 2022 as the law applied it, with the earnings leg suspended, and it no longer lets another version replace a main version that fails. We recorded it in a dated section of the analysis plan, committed on its own on 6 October 2026, before any score under it was computed.

The first rule would have taken, after a failure of the main version, the passing version with the lowest average ratio of its scores to the main version’s; on the second rule’s scores that is Monthly, two lags, which passes, so the first rule’s fallback would have authorised an average over its paths. The second rule instead allows only a passing main version to supply an average, and if the main version fails the analysis reports scenarios only. Whatever the verdict, it keeps four scenarios: the OBR central path, the OBR long-term path, the historical replay and the paired paths with earnings growth 0.5 points lower and higher. Every other element, including all thresholds in Table 3, is as first registered.

With the data available at the October 2026 Budget, the main version failed one condition (Table 9, Table 10). It expected earnings growth below 2.5% in 44.6% of the years tested on the seven-forecast test, against 17.9% in practice, about 2.5 times as often, on an event that sets dt>0d_t > 0. That average error, 0.267, exceeds the limit of 0.25 by 0.017; the main version would pass at a limit of 0.27, with every other element of the rule unchanged. The error is 2.6 standard errors from zero, using a Newey–West standard error of 0.101 with three lags over the seven overlapping windows. The rule compares point estimates with the limit, and the 95% interval, 0.07 to 0.46, contains it. All five versions share the error, with floor biases from 0.243 to 0.275. The main version also expected earnings growth to exceed CPI by 1.00 points more than it did (2.3 standard errors from zero), inside the limit of 1.5 and in the same direction for every version, against 0.29 with April 2022 as published. Taken together with the floor bias, this says the paths place earnings growth too high on average and too often below 2.5%, that is, too dispersed, a cause the versions share; centring every path on the OBR’s point forecasts and the shared pool of residuals both bear on it, and the test does not separate them.

The main version passed every other condition. The outcome fell inside its central 80% band in five of seven four-year windows and 83% of annual checks, and its lead changes were close to those realised. On the published 2022 earnings, a sensitivity that does not enter the verdict, it expected 1.29 changes of lead per window against 1.86. The realised gap since 2011 sits at the 55th percentile of its paths. Two other versions passed and two failed on the same condition, but the rule forbids promoting another version, so this paper gives no expected value.

The test has little power. With seven overlapping four-year windows, a model whose bands cover far less than 80% could still pass, and because the upper limit on coverage is 100%, the coverage condition cannot detect bands that are too wide. A third registered rule applies only to estimates that use OBR forecasts published after this paper’s inputs, and adds a test on outcomes not yet observed. On simulated outcomes a correct model passes that test 35.8% of the time after four years, 68.5% after eight and 85.6% after twelve, and a model that states double the true rate of earnings growth below the floor passes 35.1%, 43.1% and 63.7%, so four years cannot tell the two apart. That rule gives its first verdict after eight years of outcomes, from 2027 to 2034, so the first test of whether averages over the paths may be reported as estimates can be made in October 2034, when the last of those inputs, September CPI, is published.

On the OBR long-term path the saving is £10.9bn in 2039-40, £7.3bn once income tax and benefits respond: extra Pension Credit offsets £0.8bn and lower income tax £2.4bn. The adjusted rule’s level on this path is the same as on the central path, £18,840 a year for the full new State Pension in 2039-40, because the two runs share their inputs; only the triple lock differs. This scenario bounds the saving from above under the OBR’s convention, and only under it: the saving if the OBR’s premium is paid in full and the adjusted rule pays none. The two earnings scenarios give £11.4bn and £11.9bn in 2039-40. They are averages over paths from the model that failed the test, carry the same failed condition, and are reported because the registered rule keeps them as scenarios whatever the verdict, not because they are more reliable than the average it withdrew. They are not a range around an unshifted case, and their midpoint is not an estimate of the withdrawn average. They exceed the OBR long-term path because on the simulated paths the triple lock’s cumulated excess over earnings is larger than the OBR’s premium gives: its average log excess over May–July earnings from April 2030, yty_t in 2039-40, is 8.78 and 5.79 log points, against 4.34 on the OBR long-term path. In April 2034 to 2039 the simulated triple lock pays 0.74 and 0.39 points a year above May–July earnings, against 0.58 on that path, so with higher earnings growth more of the excess arrives in the Aprils before the OBR’s premium is fully in. The adjusted rule removes 51% and 78% of that excess on average.

As shares of basic and new State Pension spending the two scenarios save both 4.4% in 2039-40 (log gaps of 4.48 and 4.50 points). Because they share draws, shocks and full-run selection, their difference is measured path by path: in rule arithmetic on the shared paths, the higher-earnings scenario’s share exceeds the lower’s by 0.02 ± 0.34 points in 2039-40, which cannot be told from zero, so most of the cash difference comes from the larger pension base that faster nominal earnings growth brings. Earlier the scenarios differ. In 2034-35 the saving is £4.4bn with higher earnings growth against £3.1bn with lower, 2.2% against 1.6% of spending, a paired difference of 0.63 ± 0.16 points: larger earnings leads use up the adjusted rule’s excess over its earnings path sooner, so the increment in Section 3.3 brings the saving forward.

5.2 The OBR central path

The OBR central path is one smooth path. The floor sets the triple lock in April 2029, 2030 and 2031; from April 2032 earnings lead every year (Figure 4). The two rules differ in one year, April 2032: after the floor years the adjusted Triple Lock rises only by the floor, or by what brings it back to its earnings path, while the triple lock rises with earnings from the higher level. By 2039-40 the full new State Pension is £18,876 a year under the triple lock and £18,840 under the adjusted rule, and the saving is £0.5bn (£0.3bn net). The OBR finds the same for its forecast period: “in our current medium-term forecast an uprating of 2.5 per cent would apply under both the previous and adjusted version of the triple lock in both 2030-31 and 2031-32” (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a, para. 3.50, footnote 17).

6 Comparison with official costings

DWP costs the adjusted Triple Lock at £15bn in 2039-40 in cash terms, or £11bn in 2025-26 prices (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b), and the Budget repeats the cash figure (Section 1). It is direct spending for Great Britain, rounded to the nearest billion, so it covers £14.5bn to £15.5bn, and it leaves out tax and debt interest. DWP’s method note says that “Pensim3 uses administrative and survey data, including the Family Resources Survey, to project the population and individual pension outcomes from 2018 to 2100. The analysis in this publication covers Great Britain”, that its estimates use the ONS’s 2024-based national population projections, and that “Components of the pre-2016 State Pension that are not covered by the Triple Lock are assumed to continue to be uprated by CPI” (Department for Work and Pensions 2026b). DWP’s model is a dynamic microsimulation model of the kind Li and O’Donoghue (2013) review. The Budget states that “The government’s central forecast for the trajectory of the State Pension and how it differs from the Triple Lock is set out below” (HM Treasury 2026, Box 5.A), but it publishes neither the inputs of that trajectory nor its yearly values. The government has also said, in answer to a parliamentary question, that DWP’s analysis did not use single-year earnings or prices growth assumptions (UK Parliament 2026). DWP’s description, a path for uprating with no single-year assumptions, is closer in kind to the OBR’s average-rate convention than to a simulated path, so, as an inference, the OBR long-term path is the closest of our scenarios in method.

Our nearest figures are the gross savings in Great Britain: £0.5bn on the OBR central path, £10.6bn on the OBR long-term path, and £11.1bn and £11.6bn on the two earnings scenarios. The population also differs. DWP projects deaths, migration and State Pension entitlements, while we reweight today’s survey (Section 4.2). Our survey’s spending on the basic and new State Pension, the base the saving scales with, is 94% of DWP’s forecast for 2026-27 (Table 8), but that ratio compares our Great Britain base with a DWP base that includes State Pensions paid abroad, which DWP’s Great Britain costing leaves out. Those paid in countries with uprating agreements follow either rule, and those paid elsewhere do not rise at all. On total State Pension excluding payments abroad our survey matches 97.6% of DWP’s forecast, so the like-for-like ratio for the basic and new State Pension lies between 0.94 and about 0.98. Since the gross saving is close to (1−e−Gt)BtTL(1 - e^{-G_t})B^{\mathrm{TL}}_t, scaling our base to DWP’s raises the Great Britain figures in proportion, to £10.8bn to £11.3bn on the OBR long-term path and £11.4bn to £12.3bn on the earnings scenarios. That leaves £2.7bn to £3.6bn of DWP’s £15bn unexplained by the scenarios, 18% to 24% of it, and £3.7bn to £4.2bn on the OBR long-term path. We attribute that residual to what we do not model: later cohorts’ larger new State Pension entitlements, which our base understates, and DWP’s own unpublished uprating path.

In 2025-26 prices, deflated by the OBR central path’s CPI, the base-scaled scenario figures are £8.4bn to £9.1bn, beside DWP’s £11bn. DWP does not state its deflator, and with both its figures rounded to £1bn its implied price change from 2025-26 to 2039-40 could be anywhere from 26% to 48%, against 36% on our central path, so the real-terms comparison adds nothing to the cash one. DWP adds that its savings “should not be used to then derive further point estimates for savings in an individual year”, and its saving keeps growing, to £50bn in 2049-50; our horizon ends in 2039-40.

The OBR’s own figures measure related but different things. Its footnote on the medium-term forecast matches our OBR central path, on which the adjusted rule rises by 2.5% in April 2030 and 2031 (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a, para. 3.50, footnote 17). Its replay of 1990–2025 puts payments under the adjusted rule 1.4% lower on average than under the triple lock, an average over all years with the anchor set in 1990 (Office for Budget Responsibility 2026a, para. 3.51); the same average on our replay, which starts in April 2012, is 1.6% (Section 3.4). Its estimate that the triple lock will have added £15.5 billion a year by 2029-30 compares the triple lock with earnings uprating since 2011 (Office for Budget Responsibility 2025), a different counterfactual and year; for scale only, the adjusted rule replayed from April 2012 would have lowered UK spending by £7.8bn in 2026-27.

DWP also projects pensioner relative poverty after housing costs under the adjusted Triple Lock falling from around 14% to around 8% by 2049-50, against a poverty line projected forward with earnings. Our poverty rates (Section 5.5) are for 2039-40, use a line set each year from the simulated incomes, and compare the adjusted Triple Lock with the triple lock rather than with today, so they answer a different question.

7 Discussion

Three points bear on how max-of-indices rules are costed. First, the decomposition yt=xt+Gty_t = x_t + G_t of Section 3.3 says which part of the triple lock’s excess over earnings an earnings-anchored rule removes. Path by path, it removes all of it when price- or floor-led years are followed by enough earnings-led ones, as on both OBR paths, and none of an excess that arrives at the end of the horizon. On past inputs from April 2012 it removes 42%, and on the simulated scenarios 51% and 78% on average. Costings that put the triple lock’s excess in as a smooth premium, as the OBR’s long-term convention does, therefore attribute all of it to the ratchet; on paths that vary from year to year part of the excess is still being carried at any date. On the scenarios most of the gap comes from that variation rather than from the paths’ means (Section 5.1).

Second, the OBR’s long-term premium is a reduced-form allowance for the same effect; Section 5.1 shows why the scenario built on it is an upper bound under that convention, not a forecast.

Third, the failed condition points towards overstatement. The main version expected earnings growth below the floor in 44.6% of years against 17.9% in practice, and it expected earnings growth to exceed CPI by 1.00 points more than it did: its earnings paths are placed too high and spread too wide (Section 4.4). The forward paths carry the same features, with more floor years (Section 4.3) and more changes of lead than the test or the record show. A floor year adds to the gap only when earnings lead later, and larger earnings leads use up the excess faster, so fewer floor years with stronger earnings could in principle open the gap faster; but the reweighting diagnostic in Section 5.1, which sets the share of floor years to the realised one, lowers the scenarios’ gap on every version tried. That diagnostic is not an estimate, and a model that passed the test could still differ from it. Who bears the risk also changes: the triple lock insures pensioners against years in which prices or the floor beat earnings, permanently, at the cost of later taxpayers, while the adjusted rule returns the risk of a fall in the pension relative to earnings to pensioners until earnings catch up (Bohn 2009; Auerbach and Lee 2011).

8 Conclusion

The adjusted Triple Lock keeps the triple lock’s protection against prices and its 2.5% floor, but lets the pension fall back to its April 2029 value relative to earnings after years in which prices or the floor beat earnings. Its saving therefore depends on how often the lead passes from prices or the floor to earnings, not only on their expected growth: it grows only in earnings-led years that follow a price- or floor-led one. Replayed from April 2012 on today’s figures, it would leave the pension 6.2% below the triple lock in 2026-27. For the UK in 2039-40, on the OBR’s smooth central path it lowers State Pension spending by £0.5bn; under the OBR’s long-term triple-lock convention, which pays the OBR’s premium in full and gives the adjusted rule none, by £10.9bn, an upper bound within that convention only; and on average over simulated paths with earnings growth 0.5 points lower or higher by £11.4bn or £11.9bn, £7.8bn or £8.1bn after tax and benefits respond. These are scenarios, not a range with probabilities attached: our model of prices and earnings failed a registered test on past forecasts by overstating how often earnings growth falls below the floor, so the paper gives no expected value, and reweighting its paths to the realised frequency lowers their gap. DWP’s £15bn for Great Britain rests on a path it does not publish; scaled to DWP’s population base, our scenarios leave 18% to 24% of it unexplained, and that residual is what is not comparable like for like.

The loss falls on households that receive a State Pension, with Pension Credit and income tax making up part of it. On the OBR long-term path pensioner poverty after housing costs rises by 0.70 percentage points against the fixed line and 0.75 against the relative one. The rule would also require a change to the statutory earnings link. For any uprating rule that takes the highest of several indices, the cost of removing the ratchet depends on how the indices vary from year to year, and a test of the model of that variation decides what can be reported: here, scenarios, until the forward test of Section 4.4 can first report.

The analysis has limits a reader should weigh. It gives no expected value, and the direction of the failed condition’s effect on the scenario averages rests on a diagnostic, not an estimate. The simulated paths rest on one model of monthly prices and earnings, centred on the OBR’s forecasts, with no periods of changing volatility, and they change lead more often than the records since 2011 and since 1989, a rate not tested against the fitting window. The population is reweighted to projected totals rather than aged, its basic and new State Pension spending in Great Britain is 94% of DWP’s forecast, and projected spending in 2039-40 is not checked against the OBR’s long-term projection. Behaviour is held fixed, Housing Benefit and council tax reduction respond only for households already receiving them, and indirect taxes are left out, so the net figures count direct taxes and benefits only.

Appendix A: Example pensioners

Four hypothetical pensioners, run as households under each rule on the model path near the median gap, show how tax and benefits pass on a lower State Pension (Table 11, Figure 7); the OBR long-term run carries no example households, and the model path carries no probability weight. Each receives the full flat-rate State Pension and, unlike the survey runs, claims every benefit it is entitled to. A lower State Pension means less income tax for a pensioner above the personal allowance and more Pension Credit for one entitled to it; Pension Credit counts income after tax (State Pension Credit Regulations 2002), and council tax reduction disregards the income of a pensioner receiving the guarantee credit (Council Tax Reduction Schemes (Prescribed Requirements) (England) Regulations 2012). The last row of Table 11 gives the share of the lower pension that reaches each pensioner’s income in 2039-40.

Thresholds explain the differences, and two modelling conventions set the thresholds (Section 4). The Pension Credit guarantee rises with earnings under both rules, and the personal allowance stays at £12,570 to 2030-31 and then rises with CPI, so the full new State Pension is above the allowance and taxed once the rules can differ. With only the full new State Pension, a pensioner sits close to the guarantee after tax. Under the adjusted rule the pensioner (a) therefore receives more guarantee credit than under the triple lock in some years (2031-32, 2032-33, 2035-36 and 2039-40), and with it more council tax reduction. In those years more of the lower pension is made up, which is why the line in Figure 7 moves from year to year. The pensioner with a £15,000 private pension (b) is above every threshold and bears most of the loss, less income tax. The council tenant (c) is better off under the adjusted rule in 2039-40 because of a cliff: the lower pension takes the tenant onto the guarantee credit, which passports maximum Housing Benefit, £1,334 a year more, and full council tax reduction, and together with less income tax these more than make up the lower pension. The survey-wide results cannot reproduce such a gain, because there Housing Benefit and council tax reduction respond only for households already receiving them (Section 4). The older pensioner on the basic State Pension (d) is on the guarantee under both rules: extra guarantee credit makes up the lower pension in full, but they receive £272 less savings credit. Whether (a) and (c) reach the guarantee depends on the conventions: if the guarantee instead rose by the same percentage as the State Pension under each rule, if the personal allowance were higher or if a successor to the Pension Tax Allowance removed their income tax, they would be less likely to cross it, and (a) and (c) would lose the income tax offset.
