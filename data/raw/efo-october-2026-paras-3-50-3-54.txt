3.50 
We have incorporated the adjusted triple lock into our central medium-term forecast from 
2030-31 but it does not currently affect state pension costs over the forecast period.17 
However, the adjusted triple lock is likely to materially reduce the growth of state pension 
spending over the long term relative to the current triple lock. The exact long-term impact of 
this change will be highly dependent on the future relationship between earnings growth, 
inflation, and the 2.5 per cent floor. To illustrate this, Chart 3.4 applies earnings uprating, 
the current triple lock and the adjusted triple lock to historical outturn data for earnings and 
inflation since 1990. This shows that: 
• 
1990-2007: Average earnings growth was typically above both inflation and 2.5 per 
cent.18  As a result, the illustrative pension values under both the current triple lock and 
adjusted triple lock would have remained very close to those produced by earnings 
uprating alone. 
• 
2008-2014: Inflation or 2.5 per cent consistently exceeded earnings growth. As such, 
pensions under both the current and adjusted triple lock would have been uprated by 
the same amount each year and seen their value relative to earnings rise.  
• 
2015-2025: Earnings growth exceeded inflation or 2.5 per cent over seven of the 10 
years. These are the years in which the current and adjusted triple lock mechanisms 
would have produced different pension value outcomes: 
• 
under the current triple lock, pensions would be uprated by earnings growth in 
these years, maintaining the gaps with earnings indexation that develop when 
inflation or the 2.5 per cent targets bind. This illustrates the ratchet effect of the 
current triple lock, under which this gap can only widen over time.  
• 
under the adjusted triple lock, pensions would instead have been uprated by 
inflation or 2.5 per cent in these years, narrowing the gap with earnings 
indexation. In a scenario where earnings continued to exceed inflation and 2.5 per 
cent in subsequent years, this gap would eventually close under the adjusted triple 
lock.  
3.51 
Over the period from 1990 to 2025, the adjusted triple lock would have produced annual 
outcomes relative to earnings growth and the current triple lock that varied significantly 
across the different economic environments described. Averaging over the period as a 
whole, state pension payments would have been on average 1.4 per cent lower each year 
than under the current triple lock and 4.4 per cent higher than under earnings uprating.

3.52 
This analysis illustrates that while the adjusted triple lock will not eliminate the long-term 
cost of state pension uprating relative to earnings indexation, it is expected to reduce costs 
relative to the current triple lock. Provisional analysis based on the historical experience 
described above suggests that if the next 50 years looked similar to the last 35 years on 
average, the adjusted triple lock would reduce the projected long-term increase in debt by 
around 25 per cent of GDP in 2075-76 relative to retaining the current triple lock.19 Debt 
would be around 10 per cent of GDP higher in the long-term than under an earnings 
uprating scenario, reflecting the likelihood that there would continue to be periods where 
the inflation or 2.5 per cent uprating mechanisms would bind.

3.53 
This is an illustrative estimate of the long-term fiscal impact of the adjusted triple lock. These 
estimates depend critically on the order, level and covariation of wage growth and inflation 
over time. We intend to develop our long-term modelling approach ahead of the next FRS 
so that we can provide a fuller analysis of the adjusted triple lock mechanism and assess its 
sensitivity to different long-run economic assumptions. 
3.54 
These initial illustrative estimates suggest that the adjusted triple lock is likely to materially 
reduce future spending pressures relative to current policy. However, alongside the 
announced adjustment to the triple lock, the Government stated its intention to expand state 
provision of social care. The overall impact of these announcements on the long-term public 
finances therefore depends on the additional cost of the social care reform which, as 
discussed in paragraph 3.41, depends on the final policy design and the timing of its 
introduction.
